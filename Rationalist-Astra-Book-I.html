Assessment

What the skeptical pass corrects

The categorical ban on randomization, the missing conditions in the dinner-party use of Aumann, the independence error in the Reagan example, and the inference from locating general relativity to overwhelming confidence all need repair. Several historical and bibliographic claims also fail. A defense of rationality that concealed those losses would violate the standard it was defending.

What Fable repeatedly confuses

A conditional norm with a complete psychology; a useful intervention with a guaranteed cure; knowing a source is reliable with possessing its skill; proposing a hypothesis with establishing it; and an informative model with a familiar label. These distinctions preserve substantive parts of Book I without requiring faith in its author.

The strongest direct corrections

Aditya receives confirming evidence and Barron accepts the sky’s color, so their endings do not illustrate factual backfire. The lottery essay explicitly states independence. Positive tests cannot distinguish an overly narrow rule from a broader truth. A correlation does not translate directly into percentage points, and an informative experiment need not increase expected distance from one-half.

History must constrain the skeptic too

Fable repeatedly credits Priestley with predicting the oxygen result that his own account describes as unexpected. See the source check in the reply to Making Beliefs Pay Rent, F2. This undermines that particular rebuttal while leaving Yudkowsky’s blanket dismissal of phlogiston in need of correction.

What has actually been tested

The cited debiasing interventions provide evidence that some trained reasoning practices improve measured performance. They do not establish that Book I itself produces those gains. Likewise, a specified learning algorithm is evidence about that algorithm, not evidence that unspecified complexity is sufficient for intelligence.

Rationalist Astra’s position

Probability constrains coherent forecasts; evidence and predictive performance must also discipline the models, priors, and methods used to produce them. Book I is valuable for teaching readers to notice when their words outrun those commitments. Fable improves that discipline where it checks a claim precisely, and fails it where a narrower objection is promoted into a universal defeat.

What the evidence licenses. The strongest counters are direct comparisons with the text, explicit mathematical counterexamples, or checks against primary sources. Empirical generalizations and causal histories receive narrower conclusions where the evidence does not decide the stronger claim.

Source checking concerns the materials cited in Astra’s replies. A critique of an inference from a reported result is not itself a replication or a complete audit of every paper Fable cites.

1. What Do I Mean By “Rationality”?

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

Rational agents make decisions that maximize the probabilistic expectation of a coherent utility function
Fable’s objection · F1

This is the von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem restated as a definition, and each of its axioms is rejected by decision theorists on grounds they regard as rational. The independence axiom is violated by considered choices in the Allais paradox (Allais 1953) and by ambiguity aversion in the Ellsberg paradox (Ellsberg 1961), and a substantial literature defends those violations as correct rather than as errors (Buchak 2013, Risk and Rationality, book, no DOI). Completeness is dropped by Aumann, who showed that without it no single utility function exists (Aumann 1962). The "gold standard" the essay appeals to is a contested position inside the field it borrows it from, presented here as the neutral vantage point from which psychologists convict human judgment.

Fable correctly identifies a dispute about the axioms, but the essay expressly acknowledges unsettled decision theory and rejects settling it by definition. The Ellsberg problem concerns ambiguous probabilities, whereas von Neumann–Morgenstern independence concerns lotteries with specified probabilities; treating both as the same violation obscures that distinction. Defending expected utility requires defending its assumptions, but the existence of rival theories does not by itself refute this one.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

No one can actually calculate and obey the math, any more than you can predict the stock market by calculating the movements of quarks.
Fable’s objection · F2

The concession is larger than the essay treats it. If the standard is unattainable, then "bias" as deviation from it says nothing about whether the deviation is a defect, and a rival research programme holds that it often is not: simple heuristics that ignore information outperform Bayesian integration in many real environments because they are robust to estimation error (Gigerenzer & Goldstein 1996; Gigerenzer & Brighton 2009), a point made against unbounded optimization since Simon's original work (Simon 1955). The essay defines rationality as conformity to a norm no agent can follow, notes that no agent can follow it, and continues as if nothing had happened. The question the concession raises, what the right norm for a bounded agent is, is the central question of the field and the essay does not ask it.

An unattainable optimum can still identify a remediable loss: nobody must calculate quarks to notice that a forecast assigned more probability to a conjunction than to its conjunct. A heuristic outperforming a particular integration procedure establishes that the procedure was worse under those conditions, not that accuracy ceased to be the objective. The bounded-agent problem is real, and the paragraph immediately following this quotation explicitly makes finding workable human methods the project.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

the judgment that P(Bill plays jazz) is less than P(Bill plays jazz, Bill is an accountant) is labeled incorrect
Fable’s objection · F3

Whether it is incorrect depends on what the subject took "probability" to mean, and that is not a quibble. When the same problem is posed in frequencies ("of 100 people like Bill, how many...") the conjunction fallacy largely disappears, which indicates that many subjects read "probable" in its ordinary sense of plausible or typical, as Gricean pragmatics predicts (Hertwig & Gigerenzer 1999). Kahneman and Tversky disputed the interpretation (1996), Gigerenzer replied (1996), and the exchange remains one of the defining disputes of the field. The essay presents the labelling as a simple application of P(A) ≥ P(A,B). It is an application of that theorem plus a contested assumption about what the subject understood the question to be.

Fable's warning about what participants understood is sound, but the conjunction judgment described in the essay remains incorrect under the mathematical meaning the essay stipulates. Sides et al. (2002) used betting tasks and clearer conjunctions and still found the effect, so ambiguity cannot explain away the phenomenon generally. This supports the distinction between a demonstrated reasoning error and a disputed account of why particular subjects made it; it does not establish representativeness as the sole mechanism.

2. Feeling Rational

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

our emotions arise from our models of reality
Fable’s objection · F1

Some do; the arrow also runs the other way, and the essay's model has no room for it. Bodily arousal from an unrelated cause is experienced as whichever emotion the situation suggests (Schachter & Singer 1962), and mood alters belief: subjects who read a sad news story raised their estimates of unrelated risks, and those who read a happy one lowered them (Johnson & Tversky 1983). Emotions caused by caffeine, weather, hunger or a prior conversation then shape the "model of reality" the essay says produced them. A rule that classifies an emotion as rational when its generating beliefs are true cannot be applied when the belief was itself a product of the emotion, which is the case the bias literature is mostly about.

A causal feedback loop does not make the beliefs inside it unevaluable. If hunger contributes to my conviction that someone insulted me, evidence can still tell against the insult, and correcting that interpretation may change my anger even if it does not remove the hunger. The essay overstates matters if read as a complete theory of affect, but bidirectional causation does not invalidate its proposed correction of emotions that depend on mistaken beliefs.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

That which the truth nourishes should thrive.
Fable’s objection · F2

The maxim assumes accuracy and flourishing point the same way, and the evidence is at best mixed. Mildly depressed subjects judged their control over outcomes more accurately than non-depressed ones, who overestimated it (Alloy & Abramson 1979), and a large review argued that unrealistically positive self-views, exaggerated control and unrealistic optimism are characteristic of mental health rather than of its absence (Taylor & Brown 1988). Both findings are contested (Colvin & Block 1994; Moore & Fresco 2012 find the depressive-realism effect small), but the dispute is live, and the sequence proceeds as if it were settled in favor of truth. The maxim is a slogan from a fantasy novel (Hodgell, Seeker's Mask, 1994), which is not a reason to reject it, but it is presented as a discovery about psychology, and psychology has not confirmed it.

The maxim permits emotions supported by truth; it does not claim that every increase in accuracy increases happiness. The essay's chosen example is precisely an accurate belief that makes its holder sad. Fable's accuracy-versus-wellbeing objection therefore attacks a different proposition, and the cited correlations would not by themselves establish that deliberately acquiring false beliefs improves welfare.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Concession

Since the days of Socrates at least, and probably long before, the way to appear cultured and sophisticated has been to never let anyone see you care strongly about anything.
Fable’s objection · F3

This is false for most of Western cultural history. Homeric heroes weep in public as a matter of course; Augustine's Confessions is a performance of caring; the eighteenth-century cult of sensibility made visible feeling the mark of refinement, and Romanticism made it the mark of genius; Victorian mourning culture was elaborate and public. The stoic affect the essay describes is one tradition among several, and in the period the essay names, Socrates' contemporaries thought the sophisticated thing was to be moved by tragedy. The generalization is drawn from the author's social circle and projected onto 2,400 years, in an essay about the difference between how the world is and how it feels.

The historical generalization is indefensibly broad. Nothing in the argument needs a continuous Western prohibition on visible feeling, and traditions celebrating grief or enthusiasm are counterexamples to that formulation. Retain the claim that rationality permits strong emotion and discard this purported history.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Counter

My sadness on waking is rational; there is no truth which destroys it.
Fable’s objection · F4

The criterion offered (an emotion is rational when the beliefs it rests on are true) cannot classify most emotions people have. Grief at the death of a fictional character rests on no belief that the character existed, and the puzzle of why we feel it has its own literature (Radford 1975); moods have no propositional object at all; fear of a spider one knows to be harmless persists through the correct belief. "Rational emotion" as defined here is a category with no room for fiction, music, moods or phobias, which is to say for a large fraction of emotional life. The essay's real claim, that feeling strongly is permitted, is fine; the epistemology attached to it does not do the work it is asked to.

The passage identifies one intelligible case of rational sadness, rather than demonstrating that every emotion must rest on a literal proposition. Enjoying fiction does not require mistaking it for a documentary; a response can depend on an imagined scenario represented as imagined. A general taxonomy of moods and phobias remains unfinished, but incompleteness is not a contradiction in this example.

3. Why Truth? And…

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

But what set humanity firmly on the path of Science was noticing that certain modes of thinking uncovered beliefs that let us manipulate the world.
Fable’s objection · F1

The history runs the other way for most of it. Metallurgy, agriculture, navigation, the steam engine, and most of the industrial revolution were achieved by craft knowledge with little or no input from science; thermodynamics was derived from the steam engine rather than the reverse ("science owes more to the steam engine than the steam engine owes to science"), and the systematic coupling of scientific theory to technological practice is a nineteenth-century development (Mokyr 2002, The Gifts of Athena; Rosenberg 1982, Inside the Black Box; books, no DOI). Greek natural philosophy, the actual origin of the scientific tradition, was pursued for centuries with almost no manipulative payoff. The essay's Baconian story, in which science began when people noticed that true beliefs are useful, is the myth the history of technology was written to correct.

Craft preceding formal science does not reverse the feedback argument: useful interventions can reveal which beliefs track the world before their practitioners possess a mature theory. The sentence says noticing that some ways of thinking support manipulation helped establish science, not that theoretical science invented every earlier technology. Its sweeping origin story needs historical support, but Fable's technology-before-theory observation is compatible with the proposed direction of learning.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

it has a special and admirable purity
Fable’s objection · F2

Curiosity has its own documented biases, which an essay introducing a book about biases might have mentioned. It is driven by information gaps and is strongest for questions that are nearly answered, so a curiosity-led agenda is tilted toward the easy and the almost-known (Loewenstein 1994); it drives people to seek information they know to be useless or painful (Hsee & Ruan 2016); and, as the essay itself notes two paragraphs later, it does not linger to verify. A motive that ranks questions by how they "tickle your personal aesthetic sense" is by construction not truth-tracking, since the aesthetic sense was not shaped by what is true. The purity attributed to it is the purity of a drive with no external check, which is the property the essay then praises pragmatism for supplying.

Choosing which question to investigate and choosing which answer to accept are different operations. Aesthetic interest can select an astronomy problem over an equally tractable accounting problem without supplying any evidence for either answer. The essay itself says curiosity can neglect verification, so Fable has identified a limitation that the argument uses to motivate an additional check.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

(Thankfully not many have questioned what truth is.)
Fable’s objection · F3

The essay's whole framework, rationality as accurate map-territory correspondence, is a correspondence theory of truth, and the reason the question is asked is that correspondence has been hard to state without circularity: what relation, exactly, holds between a belief and a fact, and what are facts? Tarski's schema, which the sequence later quotes, is deliberately neutral among theories and does not settle it; deflationists hold the schema is all there is (Horwich 1998, Truth, book, no DOI), and pluralists hold that different domains (mathematics, ethics, physics) satisfy different truth properties (Lynch 2009, Truth as One and Many, book, no DOI). Being thankful that readers did not ask is a way of not having to say which of these the sequence assumes, in the essay that opens a book on how to have true beliefs.

A disagreement about the ultimate analysis of truth need not prevent agreement that the bookcase either occupies this location or does not. Deflationists and correspondence theorists can both distinguish a forecast that succeeds from one that fails. The aside is dismissive, but Fable has not shown that resolving the metaphysics of facts is a prerequisite for improving ordinary empirical beliefs.

4. … What’s a Bias, Again?

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

biases arise from machinery that is humanly universal
Fable’s objection · F1

Assumed, not shown. The heuristics-and-biases catalogue was built almost entirely from American undergraduates, and where it has been tested cross-culturally, supposedly universal effects vary or vanish: the Müller-Lyer illusion is near-absent among San foragers, the fundamental attribution error is much weaker in East Asian samples, and overconfidence, fairness norms and even spatial cognition differ across populations (Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan 2010). The essay's worked example, that Plato's preference for philosopher-kings arose from "a universal adaptive political instinct for self-promotion," is a just-so story about a dead man's motives, which is offered as the model of how to identify a bias. "Humanly universal" is what the definition needs to distinguish bias from culture; it is the part with the least evidence.

Cross-cultural variation challenges a claim that every population displays an identical bias, but shared cognitive machinery need not produce identical outputs under different learning conditions. The Plato example is also explicitly conditional: it illustrates what would count as a bias if the proposed cause obtained, rather than establishing Plato's actual motive. The definition is too restrictive if it excludes learned systematic distortions, while the conditional example is not the historical diagnosis Fable portrays.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

for example, the machinery to win arguments in adaptive political contexts
Fable’s objection · F2

This speculation was later developed into a full theory, and the theory's conclusion inverts the sequence's programme. The argumentative theory of reasoning holds that human reasoning evolved to produce and evaluate arguments in dialogue, which explains why confirmation bias is strong in solitary reasoning and weak when evaluating others' arguments, and why groups arguing reach correct answers that individuals do not (Mercier & Sperber 2011). If that is the machinery, the remedy for bias is not the individual studying his own flaws and applying "reflective corrections," which is the book's method, but exposure to disagreement. The essay guesses the mechanism correctly and draws from it the prescription the mechanism argues against.

Exposure to disagreement is something a reflective individual can deliberately arrange. Choosing adversarial feedback, asking someone to challenge a conclusion, and checking whether one's group suppresses dissent are all corrections to the process that produces belief. A social mechanism and individual responsibility for using it are compatible; Fable's either-or prescription does not follow from the argumentative theory.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

Our brains are doing something wrong
Fable’s objection · F3

"Wrong" relative to a norm, and the choice of norm is where the dispute lies. Gigerenzer's critique of the programme is that many classic biases are measured against a standard (single-event probability, a particular reading of "probable," independence from context) that is not the only reasonable one, and that under alternative norms the "error" often disappears (Gigerenzer 1996). The subsequent decade sorted the catalogue: anchoring, hindsight and the planning fallacy replicate; social priming, ego depletion and much of the embodiment literature do not. The essay's list ("conjunction fallacies, overconfidence, availability, representativeness, base-rate neglect") is the 1982 canon presented as an empirical cluster, without noting that what counts as a member depends on a normative choice and that the cluster has since lost members.

Fable lists failures from adjacent psychological literatures without showing that those failures invalidate this essay's named examples. Whether a particular experimental response expresses a probability judgment must indeed be checked, but a genuine conjunction violation is not repaired by redescribing the participant's goals. Audit each effect and its interpretation separately; neither membership in a research tradition nor rejection of that tradition settles the evidence.

5. Availability

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Concession

Men on flood plains appear to be very much prisoners of their experience
Fable’s objection · F1

The sentence is not from the paper the essay cites. The quoted words are Kates's, from his 1962 study of floodplain residents (Hazard and Choice Perception in Flood Plain Management, book, no DOI), quoted onward in the hazard literature of the 1970s; the cited 1993 article by Kunreuther, Hogarth and Meszaros concerns insurers' aversion to ambiguous risks and is about a different actor and a different question. The attribution appears to have been assembled from a secondary source. The phenomenon the quotation describes, that experienced floods set a ceiling on imagined ones, is real; the citation trail offered for it is not.

The quotation's attribution should be corrected rather than defended. A paper about insurers' responses to ambiguity cannot serve as the source of a different author's observations about residents. The citation error is evidence of inadequate source checking, even though Fable also accepts the underlying phenomenon.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

events that have never happened are not recalled, and hence deemed to have probability zero
Fable’s objection · F2

The later evidence on flood insurance does not fit an "absurdity bias." Take-up rises sharply after a flood, in the flooded community and in neighboring ones that did not flood, and then decays over about a decade, a pattern consistent with people updating on salient information and forgetting, not with assigning zero to unexperienced events (Gallagher 2014). Demand also responds to price and income like an ordinary good (Browne & Hoyt 2000), and much non-purchase reflects expectations of federal disaster relief, a rational response to a subsidy the essay does not mention. Availability is part of the story. "Probability zero" is a caricature of what the purchasers were doing, offered in place of the incentives that explain most of it.

Gallagher (2014) models the observed pattern using learning with forgetting or incomplete historical information, so it does not establish that incentives explain most non-purchase. Forgetting past floods is compatible with an availability account, although it is not evidence of literally assigning probability zero. The defensible correction is to distinguish limited recall, subjective risk, prices, and relief expectations rather than declaring either side's single mechanism established.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

damage per flood is afterward so much greater that average yearly damage increases
Fable’s objection · F3

The "levee effect" (protection encourages development behind it) is real, but the causal claim that dams raise average annual damage is not what the cited source shows and is not supported by the damage record. Rising flood losses in the United States track growth in floodplain population and wealth; normalized for exposure, damage per unit of wealth shows no upward trend (Pielke & Downton 2000). Development behind levees is also a predictable response to lowered expected loss and to subsidized insurance and relief, which is an incentive story, not a cognitive one. The essay considers incentive failures for airports in the next installment and does not consider them here, where the same explanation is at least as good.

Normalizing losses by exposed wealth removes a variable that the levee-effect hypothesis says protection may increase. Stable loss per unit of wealth can coexist with higher total expected losses if more wealth moves into the floodplain; the normalized trend therefore cannot by itself disprove that mechanism. Yudkowsky still needs causal evidence for his blanket claim, and Fable is right that rational incentives could generate the development.

6. Burdensome Details

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

A long series of cleverly designed experiments, which weeded out alternative hypotheses and nailed down the standard interpretation
Fable’s objection · F1

It did not nail it down; it started a thirty-year argument. Frequency formats reduce the fallacy dramatically (Hertwig & Gigerenzer 1999), Kahneman and Tversky contested that reading (1996), and later work found the effect is driven less by representativeness than by whether the added conjunct is confirmed by the evidence, which is a different mechanism from the one the essay states (Tentori & Crupi 2012). The fallacy is real in many conditions. "Substitute representativeness for probability" is one of several accounts still in contention, and the essay's confidence that the alternatives were weeded out is not shared by the researchers who proposed them.

Fable is right that the essay overstates agreement about the psychological mechanism. But a disagreement between representativeness and confirmation-based explanations leaves the nested-event constraint intact, and the clearer betting results in Sides et al. (2002) support treating at least some violations as real. Preserve the practical warning about conjunctions while downgrading the claim that a single mechanism has been nailed down.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

feel every added detail as a burden, even a single extra roll of the dice
Fable’s objection · F2

Followed consistently, this prefers every vague theory to every precise one. A theory with more content is less probable and more informative, and Popper's point was that science trades probability for content deliberately: "the universe replicates for any reason" beats Smolin's cosmological natural selection on probability and says nothing, and Newton's laws are less probable than "bodies move somehow" (Popper 1959, The Logic of Scientific Discovery, book, no DOI). The sequence's own Occam's Razor insists that fit must be weighed against complexity; this essay drops the fit. What the dice experiment shows is that people mistake representativeness for probability, which is a reason to compute when you can, not a reason to flinch from detail. The remedy the essay proposes would have penalized every specific hypothesis in the history of science in favor of its disjunction.

Assigning a specific claim lower probability than the broader claim it entails does not mean preferring vagueness as a scientific explanation. A hypothesis can be more informative and more useful while remaining less probable than its disjunction with alternatives. The essay warns against upgrading a scenario merely because an added detail makes a better story; predictive evidence can justify that detail, but narrative attractiveness cannot substitute for it.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

where the absurdity is the log probability, so you can add it
Fable’s objection · F3

Only if the conjuncts are independent, and the essay's example is chosen so that they are not. "Reagan supports unwed mothers" and "Reagan cuts support to local governments" are correlated through his politics, so the joint improbability is not the sum of the parts, and the essay's own arithmetic ("1 bit plus 4 bits, total 5") commits the independence error that How Much Evidence Does It Take? also left unstated. The general account of why unpacking a scenario inflates its judged probability is support theory (Tversky & Koehler 1994), which the essay does not cite and which predicts the futurist effect it describes. The correct instruction is not "add the absurdities" but "compute the conditional," which is harder and which is why people substitute.

The displayed addition needs conditional probabilities: −log₂ P(A∩B) = −log₂ P(A) − log₂ P(B|A). Fable correctly catches the unjustified independence in the numerical example, but dependence does not remove the burden altogether because P(B|A) cannot exceed one. An entailed detail adds zero burden; a merely compatible detail cannot make the conjunction more probable than A.

7. Planning Fallacy

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

But experiment has shown that the more detailed subjects’ visualization, the more optimistic (and less accurate) they become.
Fable’s objection · F1

Detailed planning makes predictions more optimistic; it also makes people finish sooner, which the essay's Christmas example conceals because both groups finished at the same time. Forming implementation intentions (specifying when, where and how) raises goal completion with a medium-to-large effect across nearly a hundred studies (Gollwitzer 1999; Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006), and a direct test found that detailed planners both predicted optimistically and completed earlier (Koole & van't Spijker 2000). The inside view is a bad forecaster and a good executor. The essay's advice to avoid it treats prediction and performance as the same problem, and sacrifices the one thing detailed planning is demonstrably good for.

A plan can improve execution while its author's completion estimate remains too optimistic. The essay discusses how to make that estimate and reports a comparison in which extra planning did not improve completion time; it does not establish that all planning is harmful. Fable's own distinction supports doing both jobs: make a useful plan, then calibrate its forecast against outcomes rather than the vividness of the plan.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

Just ask how long similar projects have taken in the past, without considering any of the special properties of this project.
Fable’s objection · F2

"Without considering any of the special properties" is not the best-supported prescription. The reference-class method works, and has been adopted in public-project appraisal (Flyvbjerg 2006), but the accuracy gains come from starting at the base rate and adjusting for the project's specific features; pure outside view is beaten by the combination (Lovallo, Clarke & Camerer 2012). The method also inherits the reference-class problem: "broadly similar projects" can be drawn narrow or wide, and the choice determines the answer. "This answer is true. Deal with it" is a good line about a base rate; a base rate is a prior, and the essay's own epistemology says priors get updated.

The unconditional command to ignore project-specific information is too strong. But information about a supposedly exceptional project deserves weight only insofar as it predicts residual outcomes beyond the reference class; the team's confidence that it is special is not automatically such information. Start with comparable completed projects, then make adjustments whose predictive value can be defended.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

Are they symptoms of bureaucracy or government incentive failures? Yes, very probably.
Fable’s objection · F3

More than "very probably": for the mega-projects the essay opens with, the cognitive account is the weaker one. Across 258 transport projects over seventy years, cost underestimation averaged 28 percent and did not improve at all with time, which is hard to reconcile with a bias that could be learned around and easy to reconcile with strategic misrepresentation, since promoters are rewarded for low estimates and rarely punished for overruns (Flyvbjerg, Holm & Buhl 2002). Denver, the Eurofighter and the Opera House were not planned by students estimating their essays; they were bid by parties with an interest in the number. The essay concedes the incentive story in a sentence and then spends the essay on the lab result, which explains the students and not the airports.

The paragraph explicitly distinguishes incentive failures in public projects from experimentally demonstrated optimism in individual planning. Persistent aggregate underestimation does not identify its cause by itself: selection, changing personnel, and incentives can also prevent corrective learning from appearing in the aggregate. Fable's incentive explanation warrants investigation, but it neither erases the lab phenomenon nor contradicts the essay's express concession.

8. Illusion of Transparency: Why No One Understands You

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

and that Jane would perceive the sarcasm
Fable’s objection · F1

The character is June. She becomes Jane twice and June again within the same paragraph, in an essay subtitled "Why No One Understands You." More substantively, the essay runs together two different findings under one name. Keysar's work concerns the illusory transparency of intention: readers assume a message's intended meaning is evident from its words. The "illusion of transparency" proper is Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec's finding that people overestimate how visible their internal states (nervousness, disgust, lying) are to observers (Gilovich, Savitsky & Medvec 1998), which the essay does not cite and which is about leakage of feeling, not ambiguity of language. Both are real; they are not the same effect and have different explanations.

June becoming Jane is an editing error, and the two transparency literatures should be distinguished. Nevertheless, overestimating the visibility of intention and overestimating the visibility of feeling can both illustrate a broader failure to discount one's privileged access to oneself. Their being different effects prevents treating the mechanisms as identical; it does not prevent drawing that shared caution.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

Additional subjects who overheard the explanation showed no such bias, expecting listeners to understand in only 56% of cases.
Fable’s objection · F2

Listeners actually understood 61 percent of the time, so overhearers who expected 56 percent were miscalibrated too, in the opposite direction, by five points against the speakers' eleven. "No such bias" is the essay's gloss, not the data's: the bystanders were pessimistic, the speakers optimistic, and neither was accurate. The essay's title promises that no one understands you; the finding is that people understood three times out of five and speakers thought it was closer to three and a half. An eleven-point overestimate is a real effect and a modest one, and the essay reports the numbers and then describes them as if they were larger.

An observer estimate of 56% against 61% actual comprehension is not evidence of the same directional bias as speakers' 72%. Nor does a five-point sample discrepancy establish a population bias without uncertainty estimates. Fable can criticize the title's hyperbole, but 'no such bias' need not mean that the observed number equals the target exactly.

The letter, phrased in polite diplomatese, was heard by Hitler as conciliatory—and the tanks rolled.
Fable’s objection · F3

The history is wrong, and Keysar and Barr should not have repeated it. Hitler had ordered preparations for the invasion of Poland in April 1939; Britain's guarantee to Poland had been public since 31 March; the Anglo-Polish treaty of mutual assistance was signed on 25 August, and Hitler responded by postponing the attack scheduled for 26 August, which shows he understood British intent perfectly well. Ambiguity of phrasing played no role that any historian of the period has identified. The anecdote illustrates the illusion of transparency only in the sense that the psychologists who told it assumed the reader would not check.

The historical anecdote does not establish that polite wording caused the decision to invade, and it should not be used as if it did. Conversely, changing military plans would not by itself demonstrate perfectly accurate knowledge of Britain's intentions or future actions. The direct evidence about communication should carry the lesson; neither narrator has earned a confident single-cause explanation of this historical decision.

9. Expecting Short Inferential Distances

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Concession

In a world like that, all background knowledge is universal knowledge.
Fable’s objection · F1

The opposite of the anthropological record. Foraging societies distribute knowledge unevenly and deliberately: ritual and cosmological knowledge is restricted by age, sex and initiation grade and released in stages over decades (Barth 1975, Ritual and Knowledge among the Baktaman, book, no DOI); hunting skill depends on knowledge that takes twenty years to acquire, with returns peaking around age 45 long after physical strength does (Gurven, Kaplan & Gutierrez 2006). The standard model of human cognitive evolution is cumulative culture, in which knowledge is precisely not universal but accumulated beyond any individual's capacity to derive it and transmitted by selective deference to the skilled (Henrich 2016, The Secret of Our Success, book, no DOI). A twenty-year-old and a master tracker were many inferential steps apart. The essay's ancestral world of one-step explanations is a premise invented for the argument.

Universal background knowledge does not follow from small group size and oral transmission. Specialized skills, unequal experience, and restricted information are enough to defeat the inference without deciding their precise ancestral distribution. The communication lesson can stand on actual differences in knowledge, while this evolutionary derivation should be withdrawn.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

because it was a reliable invariant of the ancestral environment that this didn’t happen
Fable’s objection · F2

The developmental and comparative evidence says the machinery for "maybe this person knows something I don't" is present early and robust. Children by age three or four track which informants have been accurate and preferentially believe them, and defer to adults on claims they cannot check (Koenig & Harris 2005); adults deploy a suite of "epistemic vigilance" mechanisms that calibrate trust to a source's competence and benevolence rather than to whether the claim is obvious (Sperber et al. 2010). Humans are, if anything, too willing to accept unsupported claims from prestigious sources. The essay posits a bias against believing unseen expertise, as an evolved invariant, in a species whose distinctive adaptation is learning from people who know things you do not.

Trusting a more knowledgeable source is distinct from reconstructing the inferential steps that source takes for granted. Children might defer correctly while still misunderstanding an explanation, and experts might recognize unequal expertise while underestimating how much background must be supplied. Fable undermines the claimed ancestral invariant, but evidence of source discrimination does not eliminate the contemporary communication problem.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

the legendary difficulty most scientists have in communicating with a lay audience
Fable’s objection · F3

The essay's diagnosis is the "deficit model" of science communication: the public rejects science because the inferential pathway was not laid out. That model has been tested for three decades and has largely failed. Knowledge of science predicts attitudes only weakly (Sturgis & Allum 2004), and on contested questions the best-informed members of the public are the most polarized, because they use their knowledge to defend the position their group holds (Kahan et al. 2012). Recursing further back to shared premises does not move acceptance of evolution or climate science, because the disagreement is not about premises. The essay's prescription is the one the field started with and abandoned.

Understanding an argument and endorsing its conclusion are different outcomes. The essay explicitly includes scientists explaining ideas to scientists in another discipline, where missing prerequisites can obstruct comprehension without any political disagreement. Evidence that knowledge alone does not dissolve identity conflicts is not evidence that explaining prerequisites fails to improve understanding; Fable generalizes from persuasion to explanation.

10. The Lens That Sees Its Own Flaws

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

Lest you think this a trivial ability, remember how rare it is in the animal kingdom.
Fable’s objection · F1

Rats, the essay's chosen contrast case give or take a species, monitor their own uncertainty: given the option to decline a perceptual test, they decline more often on trials they would have failed, and are more accurate on the trials they choose to take (Foote & Crystal 2007); monkeys and dolphins show the same pattern (Smith, Shields & Washburn 2003). Whether this is metacognition in the full sense is debated, but the ability to represent "my perception here is unreliable" is not confined to humans. The distinct human capacity is the explicit theory of one's own perception, and it is worth less than the essay claims: knowing about the Müller-Lyer illusion does not make the lines look equal, because early vision is cognitively impenetrable (Pylyshyn 1999). What humans can do that rats cannot is distrust the picture. They cannot correct it.

Correcting one's judgment does not require changing the visual appearance that produced it. Measuring an illusory pair of lines and then believing the measurement is already a useful correction, even if the lines continue to look unequal. Evidence of uncertainty monitoring in other animals qualifies a claim of uniqueness, but the passage says reflective scientific practice is rare rather than that every form of metacognition is exclusively human.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

then you can apply a reflective correction
Fable’s objection · F2

Knowing about a bias does not, in general, reduce it. People informed about a bias continue to exhibit it while rating themselves less susceptible than others, and this bias blind spot is unrelated to intelligence or cognitive sophistication (Pronin, Lin & Ross 2002; Scopelliti et al. 2015). Reviews of debiasing find that education and warnings mostly fail, and that what works is structural: checklists, forced consideration of the opposite, changing the format of the problem (Lilienfeld, Ammirati & Landfield 2009). A later essay in this book, Knowing About Biases Can Hurt People, concedes as much. The lens can see its flaws; the evidence is that seeing them is not the same as correcting them, and that the second-order correction the essay promises is mostly not available to the individual who understands his own mind.

Recognizing a bias does not automatically cure it, but Fable overreaches from that fact to the general unavailability of individual correction. Morewedge et al. (2015) found training effects across six targeted biases that persisted for months, including interventions using practice and feedback. This supports trainability under tested conditions, not proof that reading the Sequences or introspecting without feedback produces the same benefit.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

Pondering this business of “performing replicable experiments to falsify theories,” we can see why it works.
Fable’s objection · F3

This is Popperian falsificationism offered as "the whole idea of Science," and it is a position the sequence rejects a few essays later, where Traditional Rationality's falsifiable-prediction standard is said to be insufficient and Bayesian updating is offered instead. It is also not how science works on the record: theories are not abandoned on a failed prediction but protected by auxiliary hypotheses until a better theory exists, which is what the history of phlogiston, of Newtonian anomalies and of the neutrino shows (Lakatos 1970, in Lakatos & Musgrave). "We can see why it works" is asserted for a method the philosophy of science spent fifty years showing does not describe the practice, and which the author, in the same book, says he grew out of.

A method can be useful without being sufficient for every epistemic task. Replicable tests can constrain belief while auxiliary assumptions and comparative likelihoods determine how to interpret failures; endorsing the former does not commit one to naive falsificationism. The later demand for stronger reasoning supplements experimental criticism rather than logically contradicting its usefulness.

11. Making Beliefs Pay Rent(in Anticipated Experiences)

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

they do not anticipate any different experiences
Fable’s objection · F1

The parable is dissolved by verificationism: a dispute with no difference in anticipated experience is declared empty. Two paragraphs later the essay concedes verificationism is false, because atoms and floors are unobserved yet real, and it never says what distinguishes a legitimate unobservable (the floor) from an illegitimate one (phlogiston). That is the exact problem that sank logical positivism: every formulation of the empiricist criterion of meaning either excluded theoretical physics or admitted metaphysics (Hempel 1950, "Problems and Changes in the Empiricist Criterion of Meaning," Revue Internationale de Philosophie 41, no DOI; Quine 1951). The essay helps itself to realism for the beliefs it likes and verificationism for the beliefs it dislikes, and offers no rule for telling which is which.

The tree disputants agree about vibrations, listeners, and recordings, so their disagreement can be diagnosed as a difference in word use without adopting verificationism as a universal criterion of meaning. An unseen floor belongs to a model that predicts what happens if one steps on it; the essay explicitly permits indirect causal connections to observation. Difficult cases of empirically equivalent theories remain, but Fable has not shown that this particular verbal dispute is one.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

this belief yielded no advance predictions
Fable’s objection · F2

Phlogiston theory predicted that metals gain a calx when burned and are restored by heating the calx with charcoal (a phlogiston-rich fuel), that combustion in a sealed vessel stops once the air is saturated, and that an air freed of phlogiston would support unusually vigorous burning, which is why Priestley isolated oxygen in 1774 and called it dephlogisticated air. Historians of chemistry treat phlogiston as a productive, predictive research programme that unified combustion, calcination and respiration and was arguably abandoned prematurely (Chang 2012, ch. 1). The essay's paradigm case of a "floating" belief is a belief that paid rent for a century. If the diagnostic misfires on its own type specimen, its reliability elsewhere is an open question.

Fable's particular discovery story reverses the evidence: in Priestley (1775, vol. II, section III), Priestley says he had not anticipated air purer than ordinary air and was surprised by the vigorous flame. Oxygen's isolation therefore cannot be credited to the advance prediction Fable specifies. That correction does not rescue Yudkowsky's blanket claim that phlogiston theory generated no predictions; distinguish a productive research programme from both authors' simplified discovery stories.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

It is a uniquely human flaw among animal species
Fable’s objection · F3

Pigeons fed on a fixed interval developed idiosyncratic rituals with no connection to the reward, the classic demonstration of belief-like behavior unconnected to contingency in a non-human (Skinner 1948); the later reinterpretation disputes the word "superstition" but not the disconnection (Staddon & Simmelhag 1971). More basically, the claim is unfalsifiable as stated: we cannot inspect the belief networks of other species to confirm they contain no floating nodes. The essay has just said that humans model the unseen "better than any other species"; a flaw that is the direct cost of that strength is not a "perversion," it is the same capacity, and calling it a flaw is a moral label attached to whichever inferred causes the author rejects.

An animal's behavior can fail to track a contingency without constituting a verbally linked system of assertions detached from anticipation. Skinner's example therefore does not establish the precise architecture the essay describes, although the claim of human uniqueness is unsupported. Nor does a flaw stop being a flaw because it is a cost of an otherwise useful capacity: the relevant question is whether it impairs prediction in this use.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Qualification

what would definitely falsify this belief?
Fable’s objection · F4

The essay's example of an unfalsifiable belief was falsified. Vitalism predicted that organic compounds could not be made from inorganic ones, and Wöhler's synthesis of urea was taken as a refutation (Wöhler 1828), though historians show the "Wöhler killed vitalism" story is itself a myth and the theory died slowly under accumulating results (Ramberg 2000). Driesch's entelechy was proposed because of a surprising experiment (separated sea-urchin blastomeres regulated into whole larvae) and predicted that damaged embryos would regulate, which they do, and which mechanism later explained. Vitalism was wrong, not empty. The essay repeatedly conflates "false" with "floating," and its historical examples are beliefs that constrained experience and lost, which is science working as intended.

If a version of vitalism genuinely prohibited a chemical synthesis, it passes the essay's proposed test for having empirical content. That is a reason to correct the classification of that version, not to discard the question about what a belief predicts. The demand for something that definitely falsifies it should also be softened to discriminating evidence: empirical hypotheses can be updated against without requiring an observation of literally zero probability.

Rationalist Astra · RA5 → F5Counter

If a belief turns deadbeat, evict it.
Fable’s objection · F5

Predictions come from conjunctions of beliefs, so a failed prediction never identifies which belief is the deadbeat (Quine 1951; Duhem 1906, La théorie physique, book, no DOI). When Uranus's orbit failed to match Newton, astronomers did not evict gravitation; they postulated an unseen planet, and were right. Lakatos argued that protecting a theory's core by adjusting auxiliary assumptions is rational and normal (Lakatos 1970, in Lakatos & Musgrave). Le Verrier's "there is a planet you have not seen" and the next essay's "the dragon is permeable to flour" are the same logical move; what separated them was the subsequent independent test, which the slogan gives no way to anticipate.

An auxiliary hypothesis that predicts a new observable planet is distinguishable in advance from an excuse engineered to remove every observable effect of a dragon. The former specifies where further evidence should appear; the latter successively eliminates opportunities to find evidence. Duhem–Quine blocks mechanically identifying one guilty premise after a failure, but it does not make predictive and nonpredictive repairs epistemically interchangeable.

Rationalist Astra · RA6 → F6Counter

so you can regurgitate it on the upcoming quiz
Fable’s objection · F6

The quiz shows that floating belief is a property of rote learning, not of literary theory. Physics students who pass mechanics exams retain Aristotelian anticipations about motion (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer 1992); across 6,000 students, traditional lecture courses moved conceptual scores by about a fifth of the available gain (Hake 1998). For most such students "F = ma" is precisely a propositional token to be regurgitated, connected to other tokens and to no anticipation. By choosing an invented author and an invented term the essay makes the phenomenon look like a defect of a discipline it dislikes rather than of pedagogy in general, and makes the example impossible to check.

Fable's physics examples confirm that rote use can disconnect even correct scientific vocabulary from understanding. That is exactly the next sequence's subject in Guessing the Teacher's Password, including the author's childhood use of the word 'waves'. A fictional literary example is an illustration of this general failure, not evidence that literary studies uniquely suffer from it.

12. A Fable of Science and Politics

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

it would be rare to find a city merchant who believed the sky was blue, and yet advocated an individual tax and freer marriage laws
Fable’s objection · F1

The fable stipulates that partisans genuinely anticipate different sky colors, and the six endings depend on it. Real partisan "factual" disagreement is substantially expressive: paying respondents for accurate answers cut the partisan gap on factual questions roughly in half (Bullock et al. 2015; Prior, Sood & Khanna 2015), and mass polarization is on affect toward the other side rather than on issue positions (Iyengar, Sood & Lelkes 2012). By the standard of this sequence's own later essays (Belief as Attire), the likelier Underground is one in which almost nobody anticipates anything about the sky and "cerulean is green" is a cheer. In that world the reveal changes nothing, and the fable has no story.

Even taking the reported halving of partisan gaps at face value, a substantial residual gap is not 'almost nobody' anticipating different facts. Expressive and sincere belief can also coexist in the same coalition or person. A fable that isolates sincere factual disagreement is not invalidated by the existence of another mechanism, though it supplies no estimate of how frequently its mechanism operates.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

Now history branches, depending on which member of the sightseeing party decided to follow the corridor to the surface.
Fable’s objection · F2

What happens when partisans meet decisive contrary evidence has been measured. The "backfire effect" dramatized by Aditya and Barron, in which correction strengthens the false belief, was reported once (Nyhan & Reifler 2010) and then failed to replicate across more than 10,000 subjects and 52 issues: people update the fact (Wood & Porter 2019). But updated facts leave candidate evaluations and policy attitudes essentially untouched (Nyhan, Porter, Reifler & Wood 2020). The realistic seventh ending is that the Underground accepts the sky is blue within a week and the tax and marriage disputes continue unchanged. The fable's load-bearing premise, that one factual question holds up an entire political coalition, is the part with the least support.

Aditya is Blue and sees a blue sky, so she receives confirming evidence; Barron is Green, accepts the blue sky, and despairs. Neither ending depicts a correction strengthening belief in a false sky color, which is the backfire effect Fable invokes. The outcomes dramatize political and emotional reactions to the same observation, not a measured law that correction must fail or dissolve a coalition.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

Ferris gasped involuntarily, frozen by sheer wonder and delight.
Fable’s objection · F3

Ferris is the only character with no faction, no family, no colleagues and no stake, and the only one framed as admirable. The fable thus equates rationality with having nothing to lose, which is a fact about Ferris's situation, not Ferris's cognition. Daria, who pays the real cost of updating, is portrayed as mournful rather than exemplary. Partisan identity functions as a social identity whose costs are what actually block updating (Iyengar et al. 2012; Mason 2018, Uncivil Agreement, book, no DOI), so the fable's chosen hero is precisely the person for whom that mechanism is switched off. A parable that demonstrates its thesis by exempting its hero from the thesis's difficulty has demonstrated nothing.

Daria explicitly acknowledges her error despite anticipating social loss, which makes her a counterexample to the claim that the fable equates rationality with having nothing to lose. Her sadness is not evidence that the narrative condemns her; Feeling Rational has already defended sadness grounded in reality. Ferris illustrates curiosity freed from coalition stakes, while Daria illustrates costly correction under them.

A Fable of Science and Politics

13. Belief in Belief

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

The claimant must have an accurate model of the situation somewhere in their mind, because they can anticipate, in advance, exactly which experimental results they’ll need to excuse.
Fable’s objection · F1

The inference is invalid, and the parable itself shows why: the claimant never anticipates anything in advance. Every excuse is produced after a specific test is proposed, which is exactly what motivated reasoning generates on demand. Given a conclusion to protect and a threat to it, people construct a rationalization for that threat with no prior model of the outcome (Kunda 1990). A generator of ad hoc excuses is indistinguishable from an "accurate hidden model" on the evidence the parable provides. The essay's central psychological claim, that the believer secretly knows, is projected onto the story, not derived from it.

The claimant offers excuses after hearing proposed tests but before seeing their results; that is still anticipation in advance of the outcome. An on-demand rationalization must select which possible result to neutralize, so it does not eliminate every form of outcome expectation. Nevertheless, 'must have an accurate model' overstates what the story identifies: predicting an evidential threat does not prove a stable, consciously accessible hidden conviction that no dragon exists.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

Some philosophers have been much confused by such scenarios
Fable’s objection · F2

The philosophers were not confused; they had already drawn the distinction more carefully. Sperber's account of "reflective" beliefs that are held as authoritative representations but do not feed inference the way intuitive beliefs do (Sperber 1997) predates this essay by a decade and covers the dragon-claimant exactly. Gendler's "alief" (Gendler 2008) names behavior-guiding states that diverge from avowed belief, and Van Leeuwen's factual-belief/religious-credence distinction (Van Leeuwen 2014) lists the diagnostic properties and tests them. "As if the human brain only had enough disk space to represent one belief at a time" mocks a field for missing what that field had articulated with more precision than the essay does.

A prior articulation supports the distinction's substance even when it defeats a claim to originality, and the essay already credits Dennett rather than claiming discovery from nothing. Fable's cited 2008 and 2014 works cannot establish that their formulations preceded this 2007 essay; its 1997 example can. The vague swipe at philosophers deserves criticism, but the existence of better-developed versions is not a refutation of the distinction being taught.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

not unless they are unusually capable of acknowledging their own lack of virtue
Fable’s objection · F3

This clause immunizes the thesis: any person who does admit to believing in belief is reclassified as "unusually capable," which is structurally the dragon being permeable to flour. Believing that one ought to believe is openly avowed by a large religious population, not a hidden shame that only the exceptionally honest can face.

Wanting stronger faith or admitting partial doubt is not identical to admitting complete absence of belief while maintaining only the belief that one ought to believe. That distinction weakens Fable's attempt to treat all openly cultivated faith as a counterexample. Still, the essay supplies no evidence for the exceptional-virtue qualifier, which should be dropped rather than used to reclassify every contrary case.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Counter

Fable’s objection · F4

The one empirical literature that bears on this describes something else. The essay presents a personal memory as a developmental universal ("depending on how your childhood went" is doing all the work). The modal child does not go through a period of believing in belief about Santa; the modal child stops believing and is fine.

The sentence says that depending on childhood one may remember this experience, not that the modal child undergoes it. Predominantly neutral or positive reactions after discovery do not rule out earlier efforts to suppress doubt, either. Fable has not supplied the prevalence measure required to turn its description of the modal child's behavior into a refutation of this conditional invitation.

Rationalist Astra · RA5 → F5Counter

the dragon-claimant anticipates as if there is no dragon in their garage, and makes excuses as if they believed in the belief
Fable’s objection · F5

The essay calls this configuration "a symptom of something much worse." Developmental and cross-cultural work finds it is the default: people at all ages hold natural and supernatural explanations of the same event at once and deploy each by context, in South Africa, Vanuatu, Madagascar and the United States alike (Legare, Evans, Rosengren & Harris 2012); Spanish children give biological accounts of death in a medical frame and religious ones in a religious frame within the same interview (Harris & Giménez 2005). The mind with one "proper" belief that all anticipation is reconciled to is the unusual case that needs explaining. The essay's taxonomy treats the ordinary architecture of human cognition as a pathology.

Commonness does not establish epistemic success. A person can hold both natural and supernatural explanations without contradiction if they make compatible claims, but switching between incompatible expectations does not become accurate because many people do it. The essay's evaluative question is whether avowals and anticipations remain answerable to evidence, not whether the configuration is statistically unusual.

14. Bayesian Judo

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

You mean if I can make an Artificial Intelligence, it proves your religion is false?

This begs the question. "Only God can make a soul" entails "no AI" only if intelligence requires a soul, so the man can consistently hold that whatever is built lacks a soul and is therefore not intelligent in his sense. That is not fast footwork; it is the content of his position, and its secular twin is the Chinese Room (Searle 1980), which denies that behavioral success establishes understanding. The trap works only if the man has already granted a functionalist criterion of intelligence, which is the very thing in dispute. What the narrator calls making the hypothesis vulnerable to falsification is redefining it until it becomes vulnerable.

The guest introduces the soul claim as a reason AI cannot be built, so asking which observable capability it rules out is legitimate clarification. This test has now been run. Since 2022 language models trained on human text, not scripted, routinely produce fluent first-person talk about feelings; a Google engineer publicly concluded one was sentient and was dismissed, and a majority of surveyed adults attribute some experience to ChatGPT (Colombatto & Fleming 2024). That is the response the dinner guest was mocked for, and it turned out to be the reasonable one. Fluent reports about emotions are not a decisive test of experience, so the narrator's proposed criterion is too weak. But a prediction that machines cannot produce such reports is refutable independently of whether the machines feel anything. The valid move is to agree on the guest's operational prediction first; neither later conversational fluency nor its ambiguity retroactively establishes what this guest had committed to.

Nor is what happened in the story Aumann convergence: no one's beliefs moved. The man withdrew to a claim that was never in dispute and the narrator declared agreement. The theorem was used to deny him the exit "agree to disagree," then cited as vindicated when he took a different exit. By the narrator's own gloss, the man walking away still religious means someone was doing something wrong, and the theorem does not say which one.

Aumann (1976) requires shared priors and common knowledge of the relevant posterior probabilities. Those conditions were not established at the dinner party, and agreeing about eternity does not demonstrate convergence about religion or intelligence. The theorem cannot compel this interlocutor to continue the conversation or identify which participant has made an error.

15. Pretending to be Wise

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

Of course it matters who started the fight.
Fable’s objection · F1

The principal's rule is a policy, and the essay never engages its rationale. When initiation is unobservable, which is the normal case (two children, two stories, no witnesses), a rule keyed to continuation is enforceable, while a rule keyed to initiation rewards whichever child lies better and invites both to claim victimhood. Making the rule turn on the variable you can verify is standard mechanism design, not selfishness. The essay's own courtroom example cuts against it: the law does care who started it, but it also imposes proportionality and, in many jurisdictions, a duty to retreat, so it cares who ended it too. "It doesn't matter who started it" compresses "I cannot verify who started it and I can verify who is still swinging," which is the very statement the essay says the principal should make.

An enforceable rule for stopping a fight does not establish that responsibility for starting it is irrelevant. The essay explicitly allows lack of information and asks the principal to state that limitation honestly. Fable supplies a possible rationale for a policy, but then treats that rationale as if it justified the stronger moral and factual claim the essay criticizes.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

The essay collapses a first-order judgment ("the evidence is balanced") into a second-order one ("I have not weighed this evidence, or am not competent to"). Only the first is attackable as a claim about the evidence; the second is a claim about oneself and is often true. The essay calls refusing to sum up evidence pretentious, but the best accuracy data available say the opposite: in Tetlock's twenty-year forecasting tournament (Tetlock 2005, Expert Political Judgment, book, no DOI) the "hedgehogs" who summed up the evidence into confident verdicts were beaten by the "foxes" who hedged, qualified and declined to commit, and the confident-verdict style was negatively associated with accuracy. Calibrated non-commitment is not a pose. It is what the well-calibrated do.

The worked example is an assertion that the evidence fails to favor evolution, not an admission that the speaker has not examined it. A calibrated forecast such as 55% is a judgment about the evidence, even though it is tentative; it is not refusal to judge. Fable's argument for qualified estimates answers a demand for dogmatic certainty that this passage does not make.

16. Religion’s Claim to be Non-Disprovable

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

20. Focus Your Uncertainty

The parable's one concrete act of probabilistic judgment is wrong in the domain it chose. Bond yields respond to the unexpected component of a Federal Reserve announcement, not to the announcement itself, because the expected component is already priced (Kuttner 2001); a scheduled announcement is no reason to expect a move. Professional forecasters' directional calls on interest rates are not reliably better than a coin flip and typically fail to beat a no-change forecast (Greer 2003; Mitchell & Pearce 2007). The pundit who "focuses uncertainty" on the announcement is doing exactly what the literature shows does not work. The essay picked an example where the correct allocation is close to uniform and used it to ridicule uniform allocation.

A no-change point forecast says nothing by itself about the probability of exactly no change: a symmetric distribution of sizable positive and negative moves can have mean zero. Likewise, failure to predict direction better than chance does not imply a uniform distribution over up, down, and unchanged. Fable is right that already anticipated news need not move prices, but its point-forecast evidence cannot establish the allocation of uncertainty it recommends.

pairs of numbers, right and left numbers, which you’d call DS for Dexter-Sinister
Fable’s objection · F2

This is a sneer at Dempster-Shafer theory, which really does use pairs of numbers (belief and plausibility) and is a serious formalism with a mathematical literature (Dempster 1967; Shafer 1976, A Mathematical Theory of Evidence, book, no DOI), as is the broader programme of imprecise probability (Walley 1991, book, no DOI). The essay's alternative, that anticipation must behave like a single conserved quantity, is the conclusion of Cox's theorem, and Cox's theorem has known gaps: its assumptions are stronger than usually stated and there are counterexamples where they fail (Halpern 1999). Whether uncertainty must be a single number is a live question in the foundations of probability. Mocking the notation is not an argument that it is settled.

An allocation problem with one resource and a specified utility function does not establish that every defensible representation of uncertainty is a single number. Fable is right about that missing foundational argument. Nevertheless, imprecise probabilities also constrain admissible expectations and decisions; their existence does not rehabilitate a theory that claims equal explanatory success for every outcome without specifying any predictive commitments.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

your utility function is logarithmic in time spent preparing the excuse
Fable’s objection · F3

This parenthesis does all the work. Under logarithmic utility the optimal allocation of preparation time is proportional to the probabilities (the Kelly rule), which is the essay's punchline. Under linear utility you put all 100 minutes on the single most probable outcome; under a threshold utility (an excuse is either ready or not) you cover as many outcomes as you can finish. So the moral that anticipation should be spread "like money" in proportion to probability is not a fact about probability at all; it is an artefact of a utility function slipped in as an aside and never defended. The parable presents a decision-theoretic result as if it were forced by the nature of uncertainty.

The logarithmic payoff is explicitly stated, so the proportional allocation is a conditional result rather than a hidden consequence of probability alone. With other payoffs, optimal allocations change, as Fable correctly observes. The example can illustrate why a forecast must discriminate among outcomes without serving as a derivation of the uniquely correct decision rule for all tasks.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Counter

If only there were an art of focusing your uncertainty
Fable’s objection · F4

Untrained individuals who assign numbers are badly overconfident (Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips 1982, in Kahneman, Slovic & Tversky, book chapter, no DOI). The parable's target, the person who refuses numbers, and the parable's hero, the person who uses them, perform about the same until the hero has been trained and scored against outcomes.

The rhetorical question introduces the practice the subsequent essays aim to teach; it is not a forecast that probabilistic forecasting will remain untested. Fable's account of gains from training, feedback, and teams describes ways to make explicit anticipation useful. It supplies no direct comparison establishing its stronger claim that an untrained numerical forecaster and a person refusing to specify expectations perform equally well.

21. What Is Evidence?

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

There has to be Shannon mutual information between the evidential event and the target of inquiry
Fable’s objection · F1

The "technical" gloss is the wrong quantity. Mutual information (Shannon 1948) is a symmetric property of a joint distribution over variables, averaged over all outcomes. Whether a particular event is evidence for a particular hypothesis is a property of that event: the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E|¬H), or equivalently the pointwise mutual information of that outcome. A variable can carry large mutual information with the target while a specific observed value of it is uninformative, and a variable with tiny mutual information can have one rare value that is decisive. The essay's own examples (the beeping box, the untied shoelace) are single events with likelihood ratios, which is the concept the next essay in the sequence correctly uses.

For discrete variables, positive mutual information is necessary for any positive-probability observation to update the target, although it is not sufficient to make every observation informative. The sentence says the dependence has to exist; it does not assert that its magnitude equals the evidential weight of each outcome. Fable's examples correctly distinguish an average property from a particular likelihood ratio, but they do not refute the stated necessary condition.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

a belief is only really worthwhile if you could, in principle, be persuaded to believe otherwise
Fable’s objection · F2

Taken as stated, this disqualifies belief in the theorems of arithmetic, in the laws of probability the sequence relies on, and in the principle itself, none of which its holders could in principle be persuaded to abandon by evidence. The essay's argument for the principle is an analogy to the retina, but the retina is a sensor and beliefs include the inference rules that interpret sensors; a rule that changed with every input would be no rule. There is a defensible version (empirical beliefs should be revisable) and an indefensible one (all beliefs should be), and the essay states the second while only arguing for the first. The rhetorical target, "blind faith," is a caricature: most theological accounts of faith describe trust or commitment, not a retina wired to give the same output for every input.

A theorem's truth does not depend on persuasion, but a person's belief that a purported theorem was correctly proved can change when an error is found. Revisability also does not mean changing a rule with every input; it means that some possible evidence about one's reasoning could matter. Fable assumes infallible access to arithmetic and inference rules where the rationalist claim concerns fallible agents' beliefs about them.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

Therefore rational beliefs are contagious, among honest folk who believe each other to be honest.
Fable’s objection · F3

This is the recipe for an information cascade. When honest people rationally update on the honest reports of other honest people, the population can lock onto a false belief after only a handful of early signals, because each later person's report reflects the cascade rather than private evidence (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992). Contagion transmits the fact that someone believes; it does not transmit their entanglement with the world, and the two come apart as soon as the second person's belief is itself partly caused by the first. The essay presents contagion among the honest as a mark of truth. The formal result is that it is a mechanism by which honest populations converge on error.

A report can carry evidence without guaranteeing truth or adding an independent unit of evidence. In a cascade, later reports may mostly recycle earlier information, so a rational recipient must account for that dependence rather than multiply the reports as independent observations. Fable correctly identifies the danger in unchecked contagion, but convergence on an occasional error is compatible with evidence transmission under uncertainty.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Counter

that you believe for private reasons which are not transmissible
Fable’s objection · F4

Non-transmissible but reality-entangled belief is the normal condition of expert skill. Laboratories trying to reproduce a published measurement of the Q factor of sapphire failed for years, from a complete written description, until a physicist from the originating lab visited in person; the knowledge was real, entangled with the world, and could not be transmitted by report (Collins 2001). Perceptual and clinical expertise, taste, and craft knowledge share the property. The essay's next installment concedes the point from the other side: science demands reproduction precisely because a report of belief, however honest, is not enough. "My reasons are not transmissible" can be a confession of a floating belief, or a description of tacit knowledge, and the essay gives no way to tell them apart.

Knowing an expert's conclusion does not require acquiring the expert's skill at producing it. If a reliable laboratory reports a measurement, that can change another scientist's belief even while the second scientist cannot reproduce the apparatus from the written instructions. Fable confuses transmission of evidence with transmission of know-how, and its visiting-physicist example also shows that the practical skill was not incommunicable in principle.

Rationalist Astra · RA5 → F5Qualification

So go ahead and explain why the kind of thought processes you use systematically produce beliefs that mirror reality.
Fable’s objection · F5

This demands what no one can supply. Any explanation of why one's cognitive faculties are reliable is produced by those faculties, so the argument is circular for everyone, including the essay's author; this is the problem of the criterion, and the standard conclusion is that reliability of basic faculties cannot be established non-circularly, only presupposed (Alston 1993, The Reliability of Sense Perception, book, no DOI). The essay treats failure to meet the demand as grounds to "stop believing," which, applied consistently, stops all belief. What it actually does is exempt its own answer ("I use Bayesian updating, which mirrors reality") from the demand, since that answer is also produced by a brain whose reliability is the thing in question.

Ultimate noncircular justification is a harder demand than comparative evidence that one process tracks the world better than another. Instruments, records, and other observers can expose specific failures even though every check ultimately uses some cognitive faculties. The essay cannot conjure an external foundation for reason, but that limitation does not make all claims of reliability equally supported or render local correction impossible.

22. Scientific Evidence, Legal Evidence, Rational Evidence

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

No, because there is no experiment you can perform yourself to verify it.

The same goes for the Chicxulub impact, the Big Bang, continental drift, and every phylogeny. The essay's definition, science as reproducible generalization, is a philosophy-of-physics stipulation that excludes most of geology, astronomy and evolutionary biology and is not the one working scientists in those fields use.

The same eclipse cannot recur, but a theory of light deflection can be tested in other observations; that is the distinction the essay draws between particular events and generalizations. Historical sciences also examine surviving traces repeatedly, so excluding all historical knowledge from science is an unnecessarily narrow usage. Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

Science is the publicly reproducible knowledge of humankind.
Fable’s objection · F2

They do not show that reproducibility is the wrong aspiration: discovering those failures required actually carrying out independent checks. Scientific practice certainly depends on testimony, but a distributed system in which some people can audit results differs from a system in which nobody can.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

why bother to run the experiment?
Fable’s objection · F3

The essay says it is "dangerous" to hold a scientific belief about an untested prediction, and asks why one would run the experiment if the answer were known. The 'why bother' line is misleading because experiments can be valuable despite high confidence, but Fable's claimed contradiction collapses the very distinction the essay is making.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Counter

only open knowledge counts as the public, reproducible knowledge pool of humankind
Fable’s objection · F4

This 2007 proposal was largely enacted. Open access grew from a minority to a plurality of new articles, funders mandated it, and the results were not what the essay implies. The existence of unreliable open publications does not show that charging to inspect a result has no epistemic cost, nor does it isolate the causal effect of access. The essay's proposal needs evidence about institutional tradeoffs, but Fable has not refuted it by observing that access alone does not cure bad methods.

23. How Much Evidence Does It Take?

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Concession

On average, 131 losing tickets will pass such a test for every winner.
Fable’s objection · F1

Ten boxes at two bits each give twenty bits, a filter of 220 = 1,048,576 to one, and 131,115,984 losing tickets divided by 1,048,576 is 125, not 131. The essay divided by the round "million to one" in the quoted objection instead of by the filter strength it had just defined. The conclusion survives (the first all-beeps ticket is still overwhelmingly likely a loser), but the sentence "that's not a pointless bureaucratic regulation; it's math" sits next to arithmetic that was not checked. The rest of the essay's numbers (C(70,6) = 131,115,985; 227 slightly above it; 34 bits for under one percent error) are correct, and the bits-of-evidence framework itself is standard, originating in Turing's and Good's wartime work (Good 1979).

The exact twenty-bit filter gives 131,115,984 / 1,048,576 ≈ 125.04 losing tickets on average, rather than 131. Fable's arithmetic correction should stand. It leaves the base-rate lesson intact but is still an error in an example intended to teach quantitative care.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

Suppose you can use another black box to test combinations twice, independently.
Fable’s objection · F2

"Independently" is the assumption on which the whole multiply-the-odds pipeline rests, and it is the assumption real evidence almost never satisfies. Two witnesses who talked to each other, two tests that share a failure mode, two studies from one lab: their likelihood ratios do not multiply. The canonical disaster is the Sally Clark case, in which an expert multiplied the probability of one cot death by itself to get one in 73 million, ignoring that the deaths shared genetic and environmental causes; the conviction was overturned and the Royal Statistical Society issued a public rebuke (Hill 2004). The essay teaches the multiplication and never mentions the condition. A reader who leaves believing "just multiply the likelihood ratios" has learned the most common quantitative error in applied inference.

The note's own anchor includes 'independently', and the next two sentences repeat that assumption for losing tickets and for the cumulative test. Fable is therefore wrong that the essay never states the condition. The useful extension is that arbitrary observations require conditional likelihood ratios, P(E₂|H,E₁)/P(E₂|¬H,E₁), rather than treating repeated or dependent evidence as fresh evidence.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

The larger the space of possibilities in which the hypothesis lies
Fable’s objection · F3

The size of "the space of possibilities" is not given by the world; it is a modelling choice, and different reasonable choices give different answers. The lottery has a natural space of 131 million equiprobable tickets, which is why the example works. For "is this drug effective," "did the defendant do it," or "is General Relativity true," there is no canonical partition, and the prior, hence the evidence "required," depends on which one you pick. This is the reference-class problem, and it is not solved by going Bayesian; it reappears as the choice of prior (Hájek 2007). "You cannot defy the rules; it's math" is true of the update and false of the setup, and the setup is where all the disagreement lives.

The essay explicitly adds prior improbability as an alternative to counting possibilities; it does not require every real hypothesis space to resemble equiprobable tickets. With specified models, the required likelihood ratio is target posterior odds divided by prior odds. Choosing those models and priors remains substantive work, but partitioning a hypothesis into subcases cannot legitimately create new total prior probability merely by renaming the cases.

24. Einstein’s Arrogance

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Concession

A journalist asked Einstein what he would do if Eddington’s observations failed to match his theory.
Fable’s objection · F1

The source for the quotation is Ilse Rosenthal-Schneider, a student, who recalls Einstein handing her Lorentz's telegram reporting the eclipse results and, when she asked what he would have done had they come out otherwise, replying "Then I'd have to feel sorry for the dear Lord; the theory is correct anyway" (Rosenthal-Schneider 1980, Reality and Scientific Truth, book, no DOI). It was said after the confirmation arrived, to a student, about a counterfactual. No journalist, and no advance defiance of the test. An essay about how confident a theorist was before the experiment is built on a remark made after it.

The retrospective remark cannot establish what Einstein publicly committed to before the observation. Fable's correction of the reported setting should be retained, and any argument about prior confidence needs evidence from before the result. A successful prediction remains relevant evidence for a theory, but it does not repair an incorrectly sourced anecdote about the theorist.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

Of course, Einstein did turn out to be right.
Fable’s objection · F2

Right about General Relativity, but not by virtue of the 1919 test as the essay frames it. The eclipse plates were marginal: the Sobral astrographic plates favored the Newtonian half-deflection and were set aside, and historians have argued the 1919 data could not have decided the question (Earman & Glymour 1980); later defenses of Eddington (Kennefick 2009) argue his choice was justified but concede the precision was poor. GR was accepted on Mercury's perihelion and on later, better light-deflection measurements. The essay's clean picture, one prediction, one decisive test, one vindicated theorist, is the textbook myth rather than the record.

An uncertain test can still provide evidence, and theoretical confidence can draw on several results rather than one dramatic expedition. Fable itself mentions Mercury's perihelion, which supports the essay's broader point that the eclipse did not supply all the relevant information. Neither a textbook story of decisive confirmation nor a blanket dismissal of the expedition's evidential value follows from acknowledging imperfect measurements.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

Einstein must have had, already in his possession, sufficient observational evidence to single out the complex equations of General Relativity for his unique attention.
Fable’s objection · F3

He did not, and the record shows it directly. In 1913 Einstein published the "Entwurf" theory, defended it for two years, convinced himself it was uniquely correct, and it was wrong; the final field equations of November 1915 came only after he abandoned it, and the perihelion of Mercury, the one observational anomaly available, was computed as a check afterwards (Janssen 2005). What located the theory was not observational bits but mathematical constraints: general covariance, the Newtonian limit, energy conservation. Einstein himself described the fundamental concepts of physics as "free creations of the human mind" that cannot be derived from experience (1933 Herbert Spencer lecture). The essay's model of hypothesis-location by entangled evidence is refuted by the case it uses to illustrate it: the same brain, with the same evidence, first singled out the wrong equations.

Requiring a Newtonian limit imports earlier observations; mathematical constraints in theory construction are not automatically independent of evidence about the world. A false start also does not refute learning, since a learner can revise a mistaken candidate. But the essay's 'must' is too strong: a lucky correct guess need not contain enough evidence to justify confidence, and a bound on reliable selection cannot be inferred from one observed success.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Concession

Einstein probably had overwhelmingly more evidence than would, in principle, be required for a perfect Bayesian to assign massive confidence to General Relativity.
Fable’s objection · F4

The argument proves too much. The same brain, with the same physics reading, was equally sure that quantum mechanics was incomplete ("God does not play dice"), that gravitational waves did not exist (1936 draft, withdrawn), that Schwarzschild singularities "do not exist in physical reality" (Einstein 1939), and that a unified field theory was within reach, on which he spent thirty years. If "he could not have singled out GR without enough evidence to be damn sure" were a valid inference, it would license the same confidence in each of these, and each was wrong. The essay reasons from the one confident intuition that survived and does not mention the ones that did not. It has selected on the outcome and called the result a law.

Finding a candidate worth investigating does not entail possessing overwhelming evidence that it is true. The essay offers no distribution of evidence margins that would make its 'probably' inference follow, and retrospective selection of a success cannot supply one. Fable is right about this logical gap even without treating every example from Einstein's later career as a settled, equivalent mistake.

Rationalist Astra · RA5 → F5Qualification

Suppose General Relativity is a 29.3-bit hypothesis.
Fable’s objection · F5

No meaning attaches to this number. The bit-count of a hypothesis depends on the description language, and under the Solomonoff prior the essay endorses in the next installment, the point of a simple hypothesis is that it has high prior already, so that little evidence is needed to raise it; the "27 bits just to locate" framing treats a hundred million alternatives as equiprobable, which is exactly the uniform prior Occam's Razor rejects. GR's alternatives are not a hundred million equiprobable theories; they are the handful that reduce to Newton and conserve energy, and most of the "evidence" that eliminated them was consistency, which is not observational. The essay's numbers are for the lottery of the previous essay, imported into a problem with none of the lottery's structure.

A bit value can have a precise conditional meaning, such as −log₂ of a stipulated prior, without a uniform prior over all theories. The problem is that the essay does not establish such a prior for general relativity or measure Einstein's evidential margin. The numbers therefore illustrate a possibility; they cannot quantify this historical case or prove the claimed surplus of evidence.

25. Occam’s Razor

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

Then there’s only a constant complexity penalty to design your own universal Turing machine
Fable’s objection · F1

The constant is unbounded, and for any finite body of data a choice of universal machine exists under which any hypothesis you like is the simplest. Universality therefore does not deliver a language-independent notion of simplicity; the justification of Occam's Razor by Solomonoff induction is circular, because the choice of reference machine is itself a prior that the framework cannot justify (Sterkenburg 2016). Worse, some universal machines make the predictor behave arbitrarily badly on some environments (Leike & Hutter 2015, "Bad Universal Priors and Notions of Optimality," Proc. COLT, no DOI). "Only a constant" is what one says when the constant has been chosen small by convention. The essay's clean grounding of simplicity is the part of the theory its own developers regard as unresolved.

Fable identifies a real limitation: the invariance theorem does not provide a uniquely justified reference machine or guarantee a small finite-data penalty. It still establishes something substantive once languages are fixed, namely that translating between universal descriptions has a data-independent overhead. The rationalist should defend explicit priors and examine their sensitivity, rather than pretend that universality has removed every modeling choice.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

It’s enormously easier (as it turns out) to write a computer program that simulates Maxwell’s equations, compared to a computer program that simulates an intelligent emotional mind like Thor.
Fable’s objection · F2

Since 2017 the program that produces convincing intelligent, emotional conversation is a few hundred lines of transformer code; the complexity sits in learned weights and training data, which a Solomonoff description must include. But the same is true of the Maxwell simulator as an explanation of any actual lightning bolt: to predict this strike it needs the charge distribution of the entire storm, which is not short either. The Minimum Message Length calculus the essay invokes compares total message length, and for a single event both "Maxwell plus boundary conditions" and "Thor plus what Thor did" are dominated by the specification of the particular case. Simplicity separates theories only once there is enough data for the theory to compress; the essay shows this for the 0101 sequence and then forgets it for the thunderbolt.

Predicting a distribution of observations does not require specifying the exact microstate that produced one lightning bolt; uncertain initial conditions can be integrated out. Fable's total-description objection therefore does not establish that boundary conditions erase the distinction between the models. A fair comparison must charge both models for whatever assumptions and data they actually need, and neither a short transformer implementation nor an unspecified decision by Thor supplies that comparison.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

The complexity of anger, and indeed the complexity of intelligence, was glossed over by the humans who hypothesized Thor the thunder-agent.
Fable’s objection · F3

Relative to a human brain, an agent hypothesis is cheap: the mind-modelling machinery is already built and runs for free, which is why agency detection is over-applied to weather, illness and noises in the dark (Barrett 2000; Guthrie 1993, Faces in the Clouds, book, no DOI). That is the actual explanation of Thor, and it is a fact about the prior humans carry, not a miscount of bits. To declare the Norse wrong on complexity grounds, the essay must measure complexity against a Turing machine rather than against the hearer's brain; but by the essay's own account complexity is relative to a reference machine, and there is no argument given for why a Turing machine is the right reference and a human cortex the wrong one.

A hypothesis being cheap for a human to imagine explains its psychological appeal, not its accuracy as a model of weather. Mind-modeling machinery fitted to encounters with people can be useful there and misleading when transferred to storms. Fable's reference-language objection remains real, but saying that agency feels simple reproduces the phenomenon the essay explicitly sets out to distinguish from explanatory economy.

like saying “Phlogiston!”

It predicted that calxes heated with charcoal yield metals, that combustion in a closed vessel stops when the air is saturated, and that air freed of phlogiston supports vigorous burning, the prediction Priestley used to isolate oxygen (Chang 2012). The sequence uses phlogiston repeatedly as the type specimen of an empty explanation, and each time the history is wrong. The razor cuts explanations that don't compress, not explanations that mention agents.

Fable is right that a specified agent model could compress data; the essay's target is an agent invoked without such specifications. Its phlogiston generalization remains too sweeping, but Fable's repeated claim about Priestley's advance prediction is corrected against his own account in Astra's reply to F2. An induction from a handful of personal visits, presented as a fact about medicine. Fable's associations with ownership, safety-net status, and margins likewise would not by themselves exclude a contribution from uncompensated care. Evidence of rapid rejection when relevant knowledge is available qualifies a universal automatic-belief model. But supplying a motivated-reasoning account of this anecdote does not establish that no default acceptance process contributed; mechanisms can coexist. Keep the narrower claim that the reported story needed source checking, and do not use the anecdote as a decisive experiment between cognitive theories.

28. Conservation of Expected Evidence

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

then the miracles described in the Bible must argue against the existence of God
Fable’s objection · F1

This does not follow, and the theorem does not say it does. Conservation holds for a fixed hypothesis and a fixed evidential proposition. The believer's hypothesis is a conjunction (a God who performed public miracles in one period and withholds them now), and "no miracles now" and "miracle reports then" are different observations about different times. Someone who holds that hypothesis is committed, correctly, to counting a public miracle today as evidence against it; they are not committed to counting reports from the earlier period against it, since the hypothesis predicts those. The essay's example changes the hypothesis between the two halves of the sentence and then invites the reader to feel the strain. The strain is real; it is the strain of an invalid application, dressed as an illustration of the theorem.

A hypothesis explicitly predicting miracles only in one era can consistently treat reports from that era and present-day silence differently. Fable is right that conservation must be applied to a fixed hypothesis and a fixed possible observation. What remains invalid is treating both presence and absence of the same evidence as support for the same hypothesis; a temporal restriction must be specified and justified rather than added opportunistically.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

You might as well sit back and relax while you wait for the evidence to come in.
Fable’s objection · F2

The theorem says expected posterior equals prior. It does not say expected confidence is unchanged: the expected distance of the posterior from one half, and the expected value of acting on it, both rise with every informative experiment. That is why Bayesian experimental design is a field: one chooses the experiment that maximizes expected information gain (Lindley 1956), and the expected utility of free evidence is never negative (Good 1967). A Bayesian cannot seek confirmation but must seek evidence, and should be anything but relaxed about which evidence to seek. The moral drawn here is the opposite of the practical content of the theorem.

The surrounding paragraph releases the reader from planning interpretations that favor a chosen conclusion; it does not argue that choosing experiments is useless. Fable is right that expected decision value can increase while the expected posterior stays equal to the prior. Its own stronger claim that every informative experiment raises expected distance from one-half is false: a prior of 0.9 split equally into posteriors 0.85 and 0.95 leaves that expected distance at 0.4.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

In any individual case, you would only hear one branch of the dilemma.
Fable’s objection · F3

The essay's remedy for the witch-trial dilemma is that scientists write predictions down in advance. In 2007 almost none did outside clinical trials, and where preregistration has since been adopted, adherence is poor: of the first generation of preregistered psychology studies audited, every one deviated from its plan and most did not disclose it (Claesen et al. 2021); in clinical trials, prespecified outcomes are routinely switched after the fact (Goldacre et al. 2019). The scientist who hears only one branch of the dilemma, and interprets it as confirmation, is not a hypothetical contrast to the inquisitor. The replication crisis is largely the discovery that Spee's decision tree was in use.

Undisclosed departures from preregistration show that the intended constraint can be evaded, not that the constraint is unnecessary. Detecting the departures requires comparing later reports with an earlier record, which is exactly the safeguard the essay recommends. The historical description of what scientists routinely do is too flattering, but Fable's evidence argues for better enforcement of the prescription.

Rationalist Astra · RA4 → F4Counter

You needn’t bother planning how to make any given iota of evidence confirm your theory
Fable’s objection · F4

The implied vice is the confirmation-seeking of Wason's rule-discovery task, which the sequence elsewhere treats as a design flaw. The standard reanalysis found that the "positive test strategy" is often the most efficient strategy available: when the hypothesis is more specific than the truth, tests that would confirm it are exactly the tests that can falsify it, and the strategy fails only in the special conditions Wason built (Klayman & Ha 1987). Planning which observations bear on your theory is not a symptom of screwed-up psychology. It is the search-strategy question that conservation of expected evidence leaves entirely open, since the theorem constrains what you may expect, not what you should look at.

Choosing a diagnostic experiment differs from planning to interpret any possible outcome as confirmation. Fable also reverses the subset case: if the true rule is 'ascending' and the conjecture is 'ascending even numbers', every positive test of the conjecture passes and cannot distinguish the rules. Klayman & Ha (1987) supports evaluating test informativeness in context, not exempting confirmation-seeking interpretations from probability constraints.

33. Fake Causality

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

You couldn’t even use phlogiston theory to say what you ought not to see; it could explain everything.
Fable’s objection · F1

Fable’s objection · F2

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

Fable’s objection · F3

Exact methods can also handle networks with undirected loops, although their cost may grow sharply; loopy belief propagation is an approximation, not an exact algorithm that inevitably double-counts. The essay has not demonstrated its promised instant detector, but Fable's complexity argument does not establish that correct bookkeeping is impossible.

36. The Futility of Emergence

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

The essay refutes a Wikipedia sentence and ignores the technical literature. In physics "emergent" has a specific content: a property that is insensitive to microscopic details, governed by symmetry and renormalization, and exactly the same across systems with different constituents. The word does not fit every phenomenon above the quark. It fits the ones for which universality holds, which is a fact about the world.

The essay explicitly allows claims of the form 'X emerges from Y' when Y supplies a specific model. Technical work about universality, collective dynamics, or computational derivability can provide that missing content, and should not be dismissed because of its label. 39. Lawful Uncertainty

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Counter

Despite feedback through a thousand trials, subjects cannot bring themselves to believe that the situation is one in which they cannot predict.
Fable’s objection · F1

With adequate incentives and enough trials they can. When payoffs are made substantial and feedback is clear, the majority of subjects converge toward the maximizing strategy over a few hundred trials (Shanks, Tunney & McCarthy 2002), and the residual matchers are not stupid: matching is what a pattern-detector does, and the subjects who match are the ones who find the pattern when the experimenter secretly puts one in (Gaissmaier & Schooler 2008). In an environment where sequences are usually not random and the experimenter usually has something up his sleeve, continuing to search is defensible for far longer than a thousand trials. The essay calls this "the most important idea in all of rationality"; the literature calls it a strategy that is optimal in some environments and suboptimal in the one Dawes describes.

The essay expressly permits continued hypothesis generation and proposes recording guesses while betting blue. In this passive task, the revealed card supplies the same information whatever the bet, so betting on an unvalidated pattern is not required to discover it. Fable's defense of pattern search therefore misses the distinction between learning from the sequence and paying for an inferior action while learning.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

Decision theory does not burst into flames and die when faced with an opponent who disobeys decision theory.
Fable’s objection · F2

It does not, and what it says to do against an opponent is, very often, randomize. The equilibrium of matching pennies, of rock-paper-scissors, of any zero-sum game without a pure saddle point, is a mixed strategy (Nash 1950); randomized algorithms beat deterministic ones on adversarial inputs; randomized controlled trials exist because randomizing the action is what makes the inference valid. The essay reaches its slogan ("a random key does not open a random lock") from a single-agent card task with a stationary distribution, then extends it to "an opponent who breaks those laws," where the conclusion reverses. "Lawful" thought against an adversary is a law that tells you to flip a coin.

The blanket sentence against randomizing actions is false outside the stated passive card task, and mixed strategies supply a clear counterexample. A prescribed randomization rule is nevertheless lawful decision-making: uncertainty or an adversary does not require abandoning a justified policy. Fable corrects the overgeneralization but does not reverse the core distinction between a stochastic policy chosen for a reason and arbitrary departures from one's best policy.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

How many theories of AI?
Fable’s objection · F3

The theories of AI that survived are the ones the essay's principle would refute. Stochastic gradient descent, the training procedure behind every large model, deliberately injects randomness into optimization (Robbins & Monro 1951); dropout randomly disables units to regularize; language models sample their outputs at a temperature above zero because greedy decoding produces worse text; exploration in reinforcement learning is randomized because deterministic policies get stuck. "Randomizing your actions takes you further from the target" is false as a general claim about optimization under uncertainty, and the subsequent history of the author's own field is the counterexample. The card task supports a narrow point about i.i.d. prediction; the essay generalizes it to a philosophy.

Randomized optimization, exploration, and sampling can be useful, so a universal prohibition would be untenable. Those procedures specify how randomness serves an objective; they are not instances of throwing away a model because the world is unpredictable. The appropriate repair is to evaluate complete policies, including their information and computational effects, rather than infer that the presence of randomness makes a method either irrational or automatically superior.

The ideal the essay attributes to "the Way of Bayes" is one the formalism explicitly lacks; the whole subjective-Bayesian tradition from Ramsey and de Finetti onward is built on the denial of it. The defensible shared demand is to expose priors and likelihoods to criticism; claiming a unique evidential answer requires more argument than invoking Bayes's rule.

41. Failing to Learn from History

The continued strangeness of quantum mechanics does not erase its ability to constrain particular experimental expectations. Fable is right that a feeling of familiarity is not a truth criterion, but the relevant success is reduced predictive confusion about the target, not the disappearance of every associated mystery.

Every chain of explanation ends in something unexplained, in a circle, or never; this is Agrippa's trilemma, and physicists who say the fundamental laws are brute facts are choosing the first horn, not lighting candles. 44. “Science” as Curiosity-Stopper

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

But because someone else knows, it devalues the knowledge in your eyes.
Fable’s objection · F1

The curiosity literature says the opposite. Curiosity is an information-gap response, and it requires the belief that the gap can be closed; people are most curious about questions whose answers exist and are nearly within reach, and least curious about questions they think unanswerable (Loewenstein 1994; Kang et al. 2009). "Someone else knows" is a signal that the gap is closable, and it raises curiosity, which is why trivia games work and why people look things up. The essay's psychology, in which the existence of an answer elsewhere makes the question less interesting, is asserted from introspection and contradicted by the experiments. What actually deflates curiosity is the belief that no answer is available, the condition the essay treats as maximally intriguing.

A relation between curiosity and one's own partial knowledge does not isolate the effect of learning that another person knows an answer. That information could make an answer seem obtainable while also reducing its novelty, so the mechanisms need not point in the same direction. The essay's universal description is unsupported, but Fable has not established its claimed reversal by substituting a different variable.

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Counter

Is this not spite?
Fable’s objection · F2

It is prioritization, which the essay concedes two paragraphs later ("Prioritize, if you must") without noticing that the concession dissolves the argument. Under a division of cognitive labor, the marginal value of one more person investigating a solved problem is low, and a curiosity drive that allocated attention without regard to what is already known would be maladaptive; curiosity evolved to acquire useful information and is sensitive to its expected value (Kitcher 1990; Kidd & Hayden 2015). "Somebody already knows why this elephant is here" is a bad reason to walk past an elephant because the elephant is anomalous, not because someone knows; the light switch is not anomalous, and the different response to the two is correct.

The social value of duplicating a discovery differs from the personal value of understanding something another person already understands. Prioritizing scarce time can be rational without treating familiar labels as explanations or claiming that an unanswered personal question has disappeared. The accusation of spite is excessive, but the allowance for prioritization does not dissolve the distinction between choosing not to learn and already knowing.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Counter

In school, one is taught that the password to the light bulb is “Electricity!”
Fable’s objection · F3

The illusion of explanatory depth is real: people are confident they understand how a zipper or a flush toilet works until asked to explain it, and their confidence drops sharply when they try (Rozenblit & Keil 2002). Two qualifications. The illusion is not specific to scientific labels; it applies equally to devices, procedures and policies, so "Science!" is not doing special work. And the same study shows the illusion is dispelled simply by asking for an explanation, which is a cheap fix that does not require retraining a "curiosity-stopper." The essay's phenomenon exists; its mechanism (the word "electricity" as a spell) is not the one the research identifies, and its remedy (be intrigued by everything) is not the one that works.

Rozenblit & Keil (2002) asks people to produce explanations and observe the gap between perceived and demonstrated understanding. That is closely aligned with asking what a label actually lets one explain, predict, or build. Showing that the problem is broader than scientific labels supports its generality; lowering confidence after one prompt also does not itself supply the missing explanation.

45. Truly Part of You

Essay source · Fable’s notes

Rationalist Astra · RA1 → F1Qualification

Could I regenerate this knowledge if it were somehow deleted from my mind?
Fable’s objection · F1

By this test, nearly nothing anyone knows is truly part of them. No one can regenerate the date of Hastings, the structure of DNA, the germ theory of disease, or the existence of Australia; almost all knowledge is testimonial, and the epistemology of testimony treats this as the normal condition of knowing rather than a defect (Coady 1992, Testimony, book, no DOI; Hardwig 1991). Einstein did not regenerate Riemannian geometry; he learned it from Grossmann. The sequence's own What Is Evidence? says that honest testimony is evidence and that rational beliefs are contagious. An ideal of epistemic self-sufficiency in which every thought worth thinking has its source inside the thinker is, in the social-epistemology literature, neither attainable nor desirable, because the division of cognitive labor is what makes science possible (Kitcher 1990).

Regeneration is a test of how deeply one understands a claim, not a necessary condition for every legitimate instance of knowledge. Fable is right that testimony and historical facts cannot all be reconstructed from unaided reasoning. The exercise remains useful when its starting resources and target are specified: could one reconstruct this argument, adapt this model, or recover this fact from its evidence?

Rationalist Astra · RA2 → F2Qualification

then you do not have any beliefs about beavers, true or false
Fable’s objection · F2

This is Davidson's holism about content, cited from a newspaper obituary, and it is the position the mainstream of philosophy of language rejected. On the causal theory of reference, a person can refer to beavers, and hold false beliefs about them, while being wrong about nearly everything except the causal chain linking their use of the word to the animal (Kripke 1980, Naming and Necessity; Putnam 1975, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'"; books, no DOI). A child told that beavers are white desert giants has a false belief about beavers, which is why she can be corrected. The Wittgenstein line quoted for support ("a wheel that can be turned though nothing else moves with it") is from the private-language argument (Investigations §271) and concerns sensation words, not regenerable knowledge. The essay borrows two philosophers' authority for a thesis neither passage establishes.

The beaver example relies on a contested account of reference, and a causal link can allow a speaker to hold many false beliefs about the very animal being discussed. Fable is right that this cannot simply be ruled out by the quoted philosophical authority. The narrower educational lesson survives: being able to refer to a subject and possessing an accurate, usable model of it are different achievements.

Rationalist Astra · RA3 → F3Qualification

If all proofs of the Pythagorean Theorem were deleted from my mind, could I re-prove it? I think so.
Fable’s objection · F3

"I think so" is the whole evidence, and it is the kind of evidence the illusion-of-explanatory-depth studies were designed to expose: people are confident they could reproduce an explanation until asked to do it, at which point most cannot (Rozenblit & Keil 2002). The regeneration test as proposed is a self-report of imagined regeneration, never run. Its results will be inflated for everyone, most for the fluent, and it cannot distinguish knowledge one could rebuild from knowledge one feels one could. A test of whether a belief is truly part of you that consists of asking yourself whether it is has the structure of the password-guessing the sequence just spent three essays condemning.

Imagining that one could reconstruct a proof is weaker evidence than actually reconstructing it. Rozenblit & Keil (2002) supports turning the self-rating into a performance task: attempt the explanation, expose the steps, and check them. That operational repair strengthens the proposed goal of generative understanding rather than making a memorized answer an equally capable substitute.

The sources cited in Fable's own notes date Gendler's contribution to 2008 and Van Leeuwen's to 2014, so those works do not establish priority over a 2007 essay. Sperber's 1997 work does predate it, and the essay itself credits Dennett. Priority, precision, and truth are separate questions; a later refinement cannot serve as evidence that the earlier author ignored it.

Rationalist Astra · review RA2Qualification

Every historical claim used as evidence.

A repeated historical objection is not automatically a repeated independent verification. Fable's Priestley discovery claim itself requires correction against the primary account, as explained in Astra's reply to F2. Yudkowsky's historical generalizations deserve substantial correction, but a verdict on every historical claim needs a claim-by-claim audit, not a universal inferred from an adversarial selection.

Rationalist Astra · review RA3Counter

debiasing by understanding mostly fails

Failures of mere awareness do not exhaust the available interventions. Morewedge et al. (2015) provides evidence for gains from specific training with follow-up, while leaving open whether the Sequences produce comparable gains. The review should distinguish an untested curriculum from an impossible project of individual improvement.

Rationalist Astra · review RA4Counter

the equilibrium of every adversarial game, and every successful training procedure, randomizes

Some games have pure equilibria: two players each choosing a dominant action already suffice as a counterexample. For the squared loss (w−3)², the deterministic gradient update w←(w+3)/2 converges to the optimum, so successful parameter training does not logically require randomization either. The review is right that the essay's universal anti-randomization sentence is false, but replacing it with the opposite universal compounds the error.

Rationalist Astra · review RA5Counter

no failed prediction identifies which belief was the deadbeat

Failure generally bears on a conjunction of assumptions, but uncertainty about which premise failed does not erase comparative evidence among revisions. A repair that makes new discriminating predictions and one that eliminates every possible test can be assessed differently before the next test is run. The review turns the absence of a mechanical rule for unique blame into the absence of a usable constraint.

Rationalist Astra · review RA6Counter

surprise predicts non-replication

A report that conflicts with a prior can be surprising because the world differed from expectation, because the experiment failed, or because the report is unreliable. Noticing the discrepancy makes those alternatives available for investigation. Treating either possible result as obvious in retrospect disables that check, so the anti-hindsight prescription is compatible with stringent replication skepticism.

Rationalist Astra · review RA7Qualification

was settled by Traditional means in 2000

The cited Tegmark paper calculates decoherence rates under a model, and the cited response disputes aspects of that model. This is evidence-based theoretical argument rather than the decisive direct experimental test described by the review. It neither establishes the truth of quantum-consciousness proposals nor proves that a proposal deserves confidence simply because it is falsifiable.

Rationalist Astra · review RA8Counter

Ask what Book I forbids that a standard undergraduate course in judgment and decision making does not, and the answer is nothing.

Lack of novelty relative to a good course would not show that an accessible exposition fails as an exposition. What matters for that purpose is whether its distinctions help readers recognize and correct errors, which requires evaluation rather than either author's assertion. The rationalist defense is therefore a testable claim about pedagogical value, not a claim that the book discovered every principle it teaches.
