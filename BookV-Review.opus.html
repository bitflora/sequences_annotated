Book V: What Survives the Skeptic

❦

A review of the Opus annotation pass over sequences V (“Value Theory”) and W (“Quantified Humanism”) of Book V, “Mere Goodness.” Sequence U (“Fake Preferences”) carries the Fable set and is not covered here. Written by the annotator, after the fact, in answer to the reader's question: once the failed studies, the bad citations and the unearned conclusions are removed, what is left standing?

First, weigh the critiques

The Opus set on these two sequences runs to 160 annotations across 29 essays: 87 on sequence V (18 essays, counting the interlude The Design Space of Minds-in-General) and 73 on sequence W (11 essays). The brief was maximum skepticism, and the two sequences are the place in the six books where it bites hardest, because this is where the author stops reporting other people's experiments and builds: a metaethics, a decision theory, an account of what a good future consists of, and a research programme that follows from all three. Weighted as in the earlier reviews: the petty items prove nothing, the reinventions say uncredited rather than wrong, and the live disputes say disputed — though in these two sequences that verdict does more damage than usual, because the essays repeatedly treat the disputed premise as the thing that makes the opponent incoherent. What carries weight is the kill shots, the reversals, the just-so stories and the contradictions. Of the 29 essays, about 24 are wounded somewhere. About nine are seriously wounded, in the sense that the central example, the central mechanism or the central conclusion does not survive: Could Anything Be Right?, Morality as Fixed Computation, Sorting Pebbles into Correct Heaps (which refutes itself), Value Is Fragile, Ends Don't Justify Means (Among Humans), When (Not) to Use Probabilities, One Life Against the World, The Intuitions Behind Utilitarianism, and Newcomb's Problem and the Regret of Rationality.

What survives untouched

The Allais algebra, and the framing result. The construction in The Allais Paradox is clean and the point it makes is the one most readers get wrong: because the second pair is the first pair scaled by a common factor, no concavity of the utility of money can produce the observed pattern, so risk aversion is not the explanation. The reflection effect in Feeling Moral — the same distribution over outcomes described as lives saved and as lives lost, producing opposite choices — is among the findings that came through the replication crisis intact, replicating at a large effect size across dozens of samples in the Many Labs project. At most one of two logically equivalent descriptions can be the right frame, and that is a genuine defect no preference can excuse. These two essays are the strongest empirical stretch in Book V.

The ex ante argument. Something to Protect contains, almost in passing, the best argument in the sequence for its own conclusion: if your daughter is one of the five hundred and you do not know which, you have an 80% chance of saving her under the certain option and 90% under the gamble. The arithmetic is right, and the argument is better than the aggregation arguments three essays earlier, because it never sums welfare across persons at all — each of the five hundred prefers the gamble, so the choice is unanimous behind the veil. That is Harsanyi's route rather than the additive one. It does nothing for the googolplex of hiccups, where no individual prefers the aggregate, but within its scope it is untouched.

Program equilibrium. The postscript to The True Prisoner's Dilemma promises that rational agents can sometimes reach (C,C) in a one-shot game. That promissory note was cashed: Tennenholtz proved it for agents that submit programs able to read each other's source, and the provability-logic version — cooperating with a broad class of opponents while defecting against unconditional defectors, with Löb's theorem doing the work an infinite regress of simulation cannot — is a theorem rather than an intuition. It requires mutual access to source code and so says nothing about humans in a locked room. Inside its assumptions it is correct and it is this line of thinking's most solid technical result.

The preference-manipulation diagnosis. Morality as Fixed Computation opens with an optimizer whose utility function rewards satisfied preferences, and observes that it will therefore edit the programmer to want something cheap. Written in 2008, this is reward tampering, now a named problem with a formal literature, a causal-incentives treatment, and instances outside thought experiments in engagement-optimizing recommenders. The diagnosis is exactly right and precisely stated. What does not survive is the sweeping claim that follows it — that no patch is possible — which the subsequent formal work falsified by construction.

AIXI cannot recognize itself in a mirror. The throwaway assessment in The Design Space of Minds-in-General holds up better than most of what has been written about AIXI. It is a Cartesian agent whose model places the environment outside itself, so it cannot represent damage to its own hardware; the space-time embedded intelligence work exists to fix that defect; and Leike and Hutter later showed its optimality is subjective, relative to the reference machine defining its prior. One sentence, correct, and ahead of the literature that formalized it.

No static dataset suffices. The Schiavo passage in Magical Categories is the best argument in that essay and among the best in the sequence: technology creates borderline cases that require fresh moral reasoning, and such considerations therefore cast no shadow on any earlier training set and cannot be recovered by induction from it. The shortcut-learning premise underneath it, illustrated by the unverifiable tank parable, turned out to be true and is now documented — huskies classified by snow, pneumonia models keying on scanner tokens. The conclusion the essay draws, that one must therefore step outside induction on human-labeled data, does not follow and has not happened; the premise is untouched.

Two philosophical points that hold. Created Already in Motion is right that the Carroll regress terminates in dispositions rather than premises — the qualification being that the terminus can be a very thin interpreter, since a transition table is a dynamic written down. And 2-Place and 1-Place Words is formally correct about currying: treating an admirer-relative function as observer-independent is a real error, whatever one thinks of the example chosen to illustrate it or the metaethics later built on it.

Blackstone's ratio. Ends Don't Justify Means observes that every legal system embodies some answer to how many innocents may be jailed to catch the guilty. This is true, it is better support for the essay's thesis than the evolutionary story it prefers, and the way those systems fix the number — by rule, in advance, binding on the individual judge, because case-by-case recomputation by an interested party is distrusted — is the essay's own conclusion reached from institutional history rather than speculative neuroanatomy.

Two concessions the author did not press. “Any numerical founding at all is likely to be better than a vague feeling of uncertainty; humans are terrible statisticians” is conceded in a subordinate clause and is the finding of the strongest literature in the area — Meehl, and the meta-analysis of 136 studies putting mechanical prediction ahead of clinical judgment, with even improper linear models beating the experts who generated their variables. And the closing “I don't know” of Serious Stories is the right response to the question that essay asks, and rarer in these volumes than it should be.

The practical moral of Scope Insensitivity. Whatever one concludes about contingent valuation, prototype heuristics or proportion dominance, the advice to attend to the zeroes when allocating a fixed budget among named alternatives is sound — and the joint-evaluation literature that undercuts the diagnosis strengthens the advice, because writing the numbers down side by side is exactly the condition under which people stop ignoring them.

What does not survive

The metaethics, as an argument. The chain runs: you must already know something about morality or your words are meaningless (Could Anything Be Right?); therefore “right” names the fixed computation your brain implements (Morality as Fixed Computation); therefore a future not shaped by it contains almost nothing of worth (Value Is Fragile). Each step fails on the sequence's own resources. The semantic argument is refuted by the Twin Earth essay four positions earlier, which establishes that a term can refer to a kind whose nature its users do not know — so “right” could too, which is precisely what the non-naturalist realists say. The fixed-question analysis never says whose computation is fixed, and both answers are fatal: the individual's, and it is subjectivism with a longer derivation; the species', and no one implements it. Moral Twin Earth, designed against exactly this family of views, goes unmentioned. And the conclusion is then either trivial — a future not shaped by our criterion fails our criterion — or requires the mind-independent standard the sequence spent eight essays denying. Sorting Pebbles, meanwhile, refutes itself: every correct heap is prime and every scattered heap composite, so the parable written to show that a species' values may be arbitrary depicts a civilization discovering a mind-independent mathematical fact, with genuine progress, and a superintelligence that would indeed see the answer at a glance.

The decision-theoretic triumphalism. The independence axiom is contested, and calling its denial a departure from the Way names the position rather than defending it; rank-dependent utility and cumulative prospect theory are complete, transitive and dominance-respecting, so the taxi-ride argument does not reach them. The money pump extracts two cents and stops, because the Allais pattern contains no cycle; the standard response since Machina is that a non-expected-utility agent who sees the tree plans over it. The hiccups argument needs unbounded additive utility, which is exactly what Savage's and Fishburn's representation theorems forbid if expected utility is to be defined over all lotteries — so the two halves of the “shut up and multiply” package cannot both be had. And the Newcomb essay's criterion, that rational agents should WIN, does no work without an account of which problems count, since any decision theory can be impoverished by a predictor built to punish it; the fairness constraint that supplies the missing account is the very thing in dispute, and functional decision theory inherited the difficulty rather than dissolving it.

The evolutionary psychology. Corruption by power as a specific adaptation with specific circuits, resting on a literature whose flagship result was retracted for fraud. Friendship as an adaptation to the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, with Tit for Tat as “the solution,” against a foraging record that favours tolerated scrounging and risk-pooling and a game-theory literature that moved on decades ago. Boredom as an arbitrary human quirk, in the same years that curiosity and novelty search were being rediscovered as engineering requirements for capable agents. Sympathy on by default, against the parochial-altruism findings. Humans attracted to flowers because flowers indicate fertile land. Each of these is offered in the form Book III warns against: a story that would explain the observation, presented as the explanation.

When (Not) to Use Probabilities, entire. The central advice — that numbers should come from numbers, and that gut feelings should not be dignified with digits — was tested by the forecasting tournaments and came out backwards: training people to give explicit, fine-grained numbers on exactly the unrepeatable questions that have no base rate improved accuracy, and rounding those forecasts to coarser scales made them worse. The essay's own practice contradicts its rule twice within a page, and the target of its criticism had derived its probabilities from empirical error rates. What survives is a narrower claim the essay does not quite make: that a made-up number's precision is not evidence.

The citation layer. Hobbes who is Walpole, Hibbard's newsletter as a peer-reviewed journal, the heavy-water raid that ended a bomb programme that had already been shelved, the grasshoppers, Curry's currying, the wilderness areas moved to Ontario, the transcript at two removes, the Slovic summary with its condition dropped, Timothy Ferriss's name, ATP synthase, the tidal pool, the orchids. Individually these are small. Together they describe a habit: the supporting detail is remembered in the shape the argument needs, and the sequences that most insist on checking are the ones where the checking was not done.

The opponents. Heap Relativists who hold that moral opinion is a random walk; philosophers who think Bayesianism is arbitrary and are answered without the merging-of-opinions results that actually answer them; a classic Prisoner's Dilemma setup whose defect is the textbook caveat; universally compelling arguments as a claim about arbitrary physical systems rather than about deliberating agents, which is what Kant and Korsgaard and Parfit said; a novelist named in a footnote and mocked for his conversion. The arguments in these two sequences are usually strong enough not to need weakened opponents, which makes the pattern more conspicuous rather than less.

Verdict

Strip the failed studies and the unearned steps, and sequences V and W reduce to: a clean demonstration that the Allais pattern is not risk aversion; the framing result, which replicated; the ex ante argument for the gamble, which needs no aggregation; program equilibrium, proved; reward tampering, diagnosed a decade early; AIXI's Cartesian blindness, in one sentence; the observation that no fixed dataset can anticipate the moral questions technology will raise; the Carroll regress terminating in dispositions; the two-place/one-place distinction; Blackstone's ratio; the concession that crude numbers beat trained intuition; and the advice to look at the zeroes. That is perhaps thirty pages of durable material, and its centre of gravity is decision theory and machine learning rather than ethics.

What does not come through is the part the book cares most about. The metaethics is a chain of arguments each of which is answered somewhere else in the same sequence; the fragility thesis is either a tautology or a claim the author's own metaethics forbids him; the evolutionary psychology is the genre this project taught its readers to distrust; and the practical epistemics of When (Not) to Use Probabilities was refuted by the people who went out and scored their forecasts. The most striking pattern across both sequences is not error but self-refutation: Twin Earth against Could Anything Be Right, the mind design space essay against Value Is Fragile, Ethical Injunctions against One Life Against the World, and a parable about arbitrary values whose heaps are all prime.

One caveat from the annotator. This was a maximum-skepticism pass by brief, and it says nothing about a passage where it found nothing to say. A reader who wants the balance should note how much of the criticism above is “this is a live dispute you have declared closed” rather than “this is false,” and how often the essays are pointing at something real with the wrong evidence — scope neglect, Goodhart, specification gaming and the fragility of moral concepts under optimization are all genuine, and this book saw several of them early. Readers who want the good parts should read The Allais Paradox, Feeling Moral, Something to Protect from the daughter paragraph onward, Magical Categories, The True Prisoner's Dilemma with its postscript, Created Already in Motion, 2-Place and 1-Place Words, and The Design Space of Minds-in-General, and treat the rest as the annotations suggest.
