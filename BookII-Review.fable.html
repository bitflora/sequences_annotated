Book II: What Survives the Skeptic

❦

A review of the Fable annotation pass over Book II, “How to Actually Change Your Mind.” Written by the annotator, after the fact, in answer to the question: once the bad science and the unwarranted conclusions are removed, what remains?

First, weigh the critiques

The Fable set on Book II runs to 233 annotations across 85 essays in sequences E through K. Book II is a different kind of book from Book I. Book I was mostly about probability and the history of science, and its failures were failures of history. Book II is mostly about the psychology of belief, and it was written in 2007 and 2008 on top of the social psychology that the replication crisis, three years later, took apart. Its failures are failures of evidence, and they are more numerous, because there was more evidence to fail. Sorted by kind:

Weighted as in the Book I review: the petty items prove nothing, the hindsight items test the method rather than the author, the live disputes say disputed, not wrong. The categories that carry weight are the kill shots, the reversals, the reasoning flaws and the contradictions, and together they touch every sequence. About sixty of the eighty-five essays are wounded. About twenty-five are seriously wounded, in the sense that the essay’s central example or central mechanism is gone.

What survives untouched

The theorems, again. Conservation of expected evidence and its corollary, that a known destination means you are already there. Screening off and d-separation, correctly explained in Argument Screens Off Authority before the essay overreaches. The coin-report and Monty Hall analysis in What Evidence Filtered Evidence, which is the right way to think about selective reporting even though the essay’s intuition about its consequences was wrong. The likelihood ratio as the unit of evidence. The Fable notes attack the applications and the glosses; the mathematics is not touched and could not be.

A shorter list of robust effects. Anchoring, with a debiasing procedure that works. Evaluability and preference reversal between joint and separate evaluation. Denominator neglect and the ratio bias. The halo effect, narrowed to the traits the meta-analysis supports. Conformity, understood as Deutsch and Gerard understood it in 1955. The correspondence bias for others, minus the self half. False consensus, minus the part Dawes showed is Bayesian. Belief perseverance. Group polarization, which is the real mechanism behind three of the death-spiral essays. Pluralistic ignorance. The identifiable-victim effect, which the book demonstrates on its readers without naming. Motivated reasoning as Kunda described it, with the standing caveat that asymmetric scrutiny alone does not prove motive.

About eight procedures with evidence behind them. Consider the opposite, for anchoring and for biased assimilation. Split the Great Idea into parts and assess them separately. Plan the retreat: mentally contrast the feared outcome with what you would do, which is Oettingen’s finding and cognitive therapy’s. Hold off on proposing solutions, in groups. Raise concerns as questions or shared problems, which the aviation and surgical literatures found works and the essay on expressing concerns said was impossible. Argue the object level rather than the rationality of the speaker. Decompose global judgments and combine mechanically. Treat every additional nice claim as a burdensome detail, which is a fair paraphrase of what conjunction means. These are the book’s durable contribution, and it is notable that all of them are either mechanical or social; none is an act of solitary vigilance.

Two rules stated without qualification that deserve none. “Bad argument gets counterargument. Does not get bullet.” And: “It is superior to argue physics than rationality.” The second is the more important, because it is the standard by which most of the book fails.

The self-directed warning against motivated reasoning. The Bottom Line, Rationalization, and Fake Justification are wrong about the evidential value of other people’s arguments and right about one’s own. A conclusion written before the evidence was examined is not caused by the evidence. That sentence survives, and it is the sentence the book most needed.

What does not survive

Every historical claim used as evidence, again. The Inquisition, Fisher, Kelvin, Bacon, the Jesuits, the Nazis, Jesus of Nazareth, Gandhi, Kekulé, the Book of Esther, the Jehovah’s Witnesses, Long-Term Capital Management, the witch-burners of “a few centuries ago,” and phlogiston. As in Book I, not one is right as stated, and the pattern is the same: history remembered in the shape the argument requires. The Book II specimens are worse because several were checkable in the essay’s own source. Lowenstein’s book says what happened to LTCM; the Eagly meta-analysis says what the halo does to honesty; the Spiritual Exercises say what Jesuits do with doubt.

The psychology of the outgroup. This is Book II’s characteristic failure, and it has no Book I counterpart. Creationists lie. Theists who have thought about it have minds like Escher paintings. Scientologists have lost their sanity. New Agers are made crazier by quantum mechanics. Relativists do not exist. The lottery buyer has dropped the line of reasoning. The person who says “still a chance” is an unenlightened one. Hijackers are heroes of their own story, but Jesus Campers are insane. In every case a diagnosis is offered where an argument was owed, and in every case the diagnosis is the kind that the book’s own rules, hug the query, do not psychoanalyze, argue against the strongest form, forbid. The book warns against horns and gives its examples horns. Remove the diagnoses and about a third of the book’s pages are blank.

The priming foundation. Priming and Contamination, Do We Believe Everything We’re Told, Cached Thoughts, and We Change Our Minds Less Often Than We Think form a cluster whose thesis is that a fleeting exposure fixes the bottom line. The cluster rests on behavioral priming, which collapsed; on the Spinozan model, which was narrowed to a load artifact; on a Wansink citation; and on a misreading of a study that showed people underconfident about their own steadiness. What is left is belief perseverance, which is real and modest. “Once an idea gets into your head, it’s probably going to stay there” was the book’s empirical spine, and it is not supported.

The evolutionary just-so stories. Politics kills the mind because ancestral politics killed people. Lonely dissent is feared more than death because exile killed people. Robbers Cave is the psychology of hunter-gatherer bands. What you saw with your own eyes was true in the ancestral environment. Each is flagged by the essays themselves as speculation and then used as the explanation, and the ethnography, where it exists, contradicts the picture.

The physics of rationality. 0 And 1 Are Not Probabilities, Infinite Certainty, Absolute Authority, and No One Can Exempt You From Rationality’s Laws share a rhetoric in which Bayesian norms are laws of nature. The theorems the rhetoric leans on do not support it: Cox’s theorem has a gap and puts 0 and 1 in; regularity is impossible in infinite spaces; a 0.9999 credence in a theorem is incoherent, as the author’s institute later acknowledged; the “million statements” operationalization is frequentist and contradicts the book’s own subjectivism. The norms are good norms. They are not the Second Law, and the book’s reader is told they are.

The self-help program. Singlethink. The knife in the heart. The chant of “I can’t fool myself.” The Crisis of Faith with its allocated hours and quiet room. The Ritual with its sixty-two techniques and its tally on the wall. None was tested; all are solitary; and the evidence the book itself cites, the bias blind spot, Mercier and Sperber, the failure of awareness-based debiasing, predicts that they would not work. The book’s one honest report on the matter is in Don’t Believe You’ll Self-Deceive: does meta-level knowledge propagate to the object level? “Apparently not.” That sentence, applied to the program, is the verdict.

The thermodynamics of cults. Every cause wants to be a cult; entropy; the attractor; the air conditioner that must run constantly. It is unfalsifiable as stated, the sociology of religion finds that strictness rather than openness produces fervor, and the movement the book founded ran the air conditioner, in public and at length, with the result the essays predicted would follow from not running it. Evaporative cooling is real and was described by Hirschman in 1970. The rest is metaphor.

Verdict

Strip the bad science and Book II reduces to: the Bayesian theorems, a list of about ten biases that replicate, about eight procedures that have evidence, two rules, and one self-directed warning. That is a good forty-page pamphlet, and a more practical one than Book I’s, because the procedures are things a reader can do on Monday.

What is lost is the book’s answer to its own title. “How to actually change your mind” is answered, in the text, by an individual program of vigilance, ritual and desperate effort, conducted alone, against a mind pictured as a citadel that a single fleeting exposure has already garrisoned. On the evidence, including the evidence the book cites, minds change less by that route than by any other. They change when a procedure forces the alternative into view, when a group is structured to surface dissent, when an adversary is given the floor, when the feared outcome has been walked through, when the judgment is decomposed and the pieces scored. The book knows all of this in fragments, and each fragment is annotated here as a survivor. What it did not do is assemble the fragments into the book they imply, which would be a book about institutions and procedures rather than about the inner life of a rationalist. That book would have been shorter, less thrilling, and true.

The Book I review asked what the book forbids that a standard course in judgment and decision making does not. Book II has a sharper version. Ask what it would look like to apply the book’s own methods to the book: hug the query, argue against the strongest form, treat each nice claim as burdensome, check the citation, do not psychoanalyze the opponent, say oops. The result is this annotation set. The book passed its own test in the places listed under “survives” and failed it everywhere else, and the failures cluster exactly where the book reasons about people it disagrees with. That is the finding a reader should carry out of Book II, and it is a finding the book predicted, about other people.

One caveat from the annotator, as before. This was an adversarial pass by brief. A reader who takes the Opus and Fable sets together would land somewhere near “useful in its procedures, wrong in its psychology, careless in its history, and dependent on a social science that has since half-collapsed,” which is probably the right place to land. Readers who want the book’s good parts should read The Bottom Line, Leave a Line of Retreat, Hold Off On Proposing Solutions, Anchoring and Adjustment, Evaluability, and Resist the Happy Death Spiral, in that order, and treat the rest as the annotations suggest.
