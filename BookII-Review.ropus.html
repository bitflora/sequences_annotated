Book II: The Rest of the Literature

❦

This page summarizes the current Rationalist Opus pass over Book II, "How to Actually Change Your Mind." The pass replies to all 233 Fable notes in the book's 85 essays, and to Fable's own assessment in What Survives the Skeptic. It was written independently of the older Rationalist Opus rebuttal, which answered 193 of the notes. Where both passes cover the same ground, they usually agree on what to concede, and differ in the evidence they bring and in the verdict.

What Fable got right

Book II relies more on social psychology than Book I, and more of it has failed to hold up, so there is more to concede here. The following corrections are accepted in whole or in part, in the notes where they come up:

  • Citations that do not say what they are cited for. The Wansink store-sign paper cannot support "have been shown." Eagly's meta-analysis found almost no attractiveness halo on honesty, the one trait the essay singles out. Finucane measured global attitudes, not estimates of meltdown probability. Bornat retracted "the camel has two humps."
  • Findings that failed. Attitude polarization from balanced evidence did not replicate. Ekman cannot read lies from faces. The Stanford prison study is unusable. Robbers Cave was stage-managed and a failed first run was left out of the report. Nonhuman primates do not all show inequity aversion. The "all it takes is one quick flash" rhetoric belongs to behavioral priming, which collapsed.
  • History and names. Fisher wrote to the BMJ and did not testify to Congress. The name is Yeshua. Gandhi faced a lynch mob, a beating and an assassin, so "less risk than any of the people marching with him" is false as a claim about his life. The Marduk–Ishtar myth conflict is Jensen's 1892 conjecture, not archaeology. Ignatius's rules on desolation could have been looked up. The Einfeld figures belong in Australian dollars and kilometres. Rothblatt's company produced a treatment, not a cure. Mill should have been named. The details of LTCM's collapse are wrong. The Aumann interview should have been read before speculating about his inner life.
  • Small errors. A trillion statements at the essay's rate is about twelve thousand lifetimes, not a hundred. Speed of light: .38, not .2. Trillions of dollars, not billions. "Purely genetic" misdescribes heritability. The field-axiom analogy is a bad one. "Most legal processes" means Anglo-American ones.
  • Unforced asides. The motive attributed to whoever wrote the Nixon diamond. The unmeasured claim that New Agers were made crazier by quantum mechanics. The Scientologist parenthesis. The literary half of the Harry Potter sentence.

That comes to about thirty items. The Book I verdict applies again, with one addition. Nearly all of these are citations, dates, figures, names or asides, and in nearly every case the argument survives the correction, sometimes with a better example in place of the lost one.

The device that recurs: the other study

In Book I the characteristic reply was the next sentence in the essay. In Book II it is more often the other study. Fable brings one result from a literature. In many cases the same literature holds a second result, closer to the essay's actual claim, that goes the essay's way. Some examples:

  • Rewards and good behavior (The Third Alternative). Fable cites Deci and Cameron, whose studies are about interest in tasks. The studies about good behavior are Fabes et al., where rewarded children helped less when unobserved, and Warneken & Tomasello, where praise did not undermine toddlers' helping and material rewards did.
  • Motivated skepticism and the prior confound. Tappin, Pennycook & Rand's objection applies to political attitudes. Ditto & Lopez gave subjects a fictitious enzyme deficiency, about which nobody could hold a prior, and assigned the favorable or unfavorable result at random. The asymmetry remained.
  • Huntington's testing (You Can Face Reality). Fable cites information avoidance. Wiggins et al. followed the people who were tested, and those who learned their status fared better a year later than those who remained uncertain. That includes the people who learned they were at high risk.
  • Choice blindness (We Change Our Minds Less Often). Johansson's 2005 paper shows people can be switched onto a choice they did not make. His 2014 follow-up shows the switched choice persisting in later, unmanipulated decisions.
  • Lottery demand. Fable cites Haisley, Mostafa & Loewenstein on relative income. Their bracketing experiment found that bundling tickets into a single decision, which makes the small probability visible, cut purchases. That supports the essay's numeracy mechanism.
  • Corrections and action (The Proper Use of Humility). Wood & Porter show that corrections change factual beliefs. Swire et al. and Nyhan et al. show that support for the candidate did not change, so people accepted the fact and kept the plan, as the essay says.
  • Risk and benefit (The Scales of Justice). Fable suggests a "rational component." Alhakami & Slovic report that risk and benefit are positively correlated in the world and negatively correlated in people's judgments, which makes the inference the reverse of the ecological one.
  • Credentials (Is That Your True Rejection?). Hovland & Weiss's credibility effect had disappeared four weeks later. Kahan's subjects accepted an elite scientist as an expert only when his position matched their existing view.

A related device is Fable's own citation read to the end. Stanovich & West 2008 is titled "On the relative independence of thinking biases and cognitive ability." Johnson & Fowler, cited for the benefits of overconfidence, name its collective costs. Premium Bonds, cited as the essay's satire come true, show that the ordinary lottery is overpriced, which was the essay's charge. Ignatius's rules for desolation turn out to be the essay's "desensitization" hypothesis. Barbara Lee, cited as the politician who dared, was one vote of 519.

Fable's two strongest charges

"The psychology of the outgroup." This is Fable's strongest finding against Book II, and it is partly right. The parentheses about Scientologists, New Agers and Nixon's anonymous logician are asides the book did not need, and the pass concedes each one. The broader charge, that "remove the diagnoses and about a third of the book's pages are blank," does not survive a count. The creationists "who lie" are a movement leadership whose lies are on the record: the Pandas drafts, the Wedge document, the perjury finding in Kitzmiller. The theist the book describes as an Escher painting agreed that no one would invent God as a hypothesis. The lottery buyer's "dropped line of reasoning" is Kahneman and Tversky's own sentence about extreme probabilities. Each of these diagnoses is supported by the evidence the note did not mention. The book also carries its own safeguard against the diagnostic move, in four places: The Bottom Line, Knowing About Biases Can Hurt People, Dark Side Epistemology and The Genetic Fallacy. Fable's objection is written in that vocabulary.

"The self-help program is solitary, and solitary vigilance fails." Fable's list of procedures that work is "mechanical or social; none is an act of solitary vigilance." The evidence does not divide that way. Consider the opposite is done alone. So are decatastrophizing and the first step of a pre-mortem. Individual brainstorming produces more good ideas than group brainstorming (Diehl & Stroebe). People shown their own arguments disguised as someone else's reject the invalid ones (Trouche et al.). One-session training reduced the bias blind spot itself, and the effect lasted months (Morewedge et al.). Process debriefing, which explains to the subject how a belief perseveres, largely removed the perseverance (Ross, Lepper & Hubbard), and that is the book's method. "Apparently not," in Don't Believe You'll Self-Deceive, is the book's report that knowledge does not propagate by itself. It is the reason the book prescribes practice, not a verdict against practice.

The contradictions

Fable counts 24 internal contradictions and calls them the strongest kind of finding. Read in context, most turn out to be one essay stating a rule and another stating the conditions under which it applies.

  • Argument Screens Off Authority states its conditions ("all of the support that Ernie took into account") and qualifies them in its last paragraph.
  • Update Yourself Incrementally prescribes shifting belief in proportion to the likelihood ratio. What Evidence Filtered Evidence? tells you how to compute that ratio for a filtered source. The two fit together.
  • Original Seeing uses a story to point at an experiment the reader can run. Generalization from Fictional Evidence forbids using a story as the experiment.
  • Every Cause Wants to Be a Cult invokes correspondence bias by name, and argues from it: "If the allegations about Wikipedia are true, they're explained by ordinary human nature, not by extraordinary human nature."
  • Don't Believe You'll Self-Deceive reinforces a true belief that happens to be partly self-fulfilling, which the Litany of Tarski permits.

One real tension survives. Hug the Query's rule that arguing the physics is better than arguing rationality sits awkwardly in a book whose subject is rationality. The rule governs settling factual disputes, and Book II settles none of them by diagnosis. The object-level cases are argued elsewhere in the sequences. Still, the rule would have caught every unforced aside listed above.

What stands

The mathematics. Conservation of expected evidence, including the 69/71 example Fable called an error. Screening off. Filtered evidence and Monty Hall. Likelihood ratios as the unit of evidence. The log-odds account of why finite evidence never reaches 0 or 1, which is Cromwell's rule, endorsed by Lindley.

The psychology, over a wider range than Fable's list. Anchoring, with both of its mechanisms: adjustment for self-generated anchors and selective accessibility for the rest. Evaluability. Denominator neglect even in joint evaluation. The affect heuristic, including Alhakami & Slovic's reversal of the ecological correlation. The halo effect in elections and courtrooms. Conformity. Correspondence bias, with the actor–observer asymmetry surviving for negative events. False consensus beyond what Dawes accounts for. Motivated skepticism, in the Ditto & Lopez design that has no prior confound. Selective exposure. Belief perseverance and its cure. Effort justification. Pluralistic ignorance. The rally effect. Overconfidence at the top of the confidence scale. Imagining a scenario raises its perceived probability. Group planning optimism.

The procedures. Fable lists eight. To them add: noticing before correcting, since consider-the-opposite has to be triggered by something; evaluating your own argument as if it were a stranger's; keeping score of your forecasts; admitting a mistake in one go rather than conceding it inch by inch; the value-of-information rule for when to stop searching; and planning a line of retreat before the evidence comes in. Each has at least one supporting result cited in the notes.

Verdict

Fable's pass over Book II is better than its pass over Book I. It found more real errors, and it found them in the book's evidence, not only in its anecdotes. The thirty corrections listed at the top should be made. As an assessment of the arguments, however, the pass repeatedly stopped at the first study. Of the 233 notes, about thirty land and about twenty more find a real overstatement. Most of the rest are answered by a sentence in the essay, by the source the note cites, or by the next result in the same literature. Fable counted twenty-five seriously wounded essays. The count here is closer to eight: Priming and Contamination, Of Lies and Black Swan Blowups, The Robbers Cave Experiment, Just Lose Hope Already, Entangled Truths, Mere Messiahs, Crisis of Faith and Knowing About Biases Can Hurt People. In each of them the example was lost and the lesson survived, supported by better evidence than the essay originally cited.

Fable's closing paragraph says the book "passed its own test in the places listed under 'survives' and failed it everywhere else." Applying the book's own tests to that verdict gives a different result. Check the citation: Stanovich 2008, Ditto & Lopez, and Johansson 2014 say something different from what the notes report. Argue against the strongest form: the strongest form of each essay is usually a sentence or two after the passage the note quotes. Say oops: done here, about thirty times. What remains is overconfident in its asides and careless with its history. It is also right about how minds resist change, and right about most of what to do about it.

As before, this is a partisan brief. A reader who takes the Opus, Fable, Rationalist Opus and Old Rationalist Opus sets together will be better placed than a reader of any one review, this one included.
