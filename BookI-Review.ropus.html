Book I: Read the Next Sentence

❦

This is a summary of the current Rationalist Opus pass over Book I, "Map and Territory." The pass replies to every one of the 161 Fable notes in the 44 essays that carry them, and to Fable's own assessment in What Survives the Skeptic. It was written independently of the older Rationalist Opus rebuttal, which answered 126 of the notes; the two reach similar verdicts by partly different routes.

What Fable got right

These points are conceded, in whole or in part, in the notes where they arise:

  • The floodplain sentence in Availability is from Kates, not from the Kunreuther paper cited for it.
  • In Illusion of Transparency, "June" becomes "Jane" partway through.
  • Hitler was not fooled by Chamberlain's polite letter. The anecdote should go.
  • Twenty bits of evidence against 131,115,984 losing tickets leaves 125 survivors, not 131.
  • Einstein's "sorry for the dear Lord" was said to a student after the eclipse telegram arrived, not to a journalist beforehand.
  • Psalms 8, 19 and 104 and Job 38–41 contain exactly the wonder at creation that "not one single passage of the Old Testament" says is absent.
  • The names are misspelled in three places: Myers, Molière and Paulo Freire.
  • "Enforced religious tolerance" overstates Roman policy.
  • The aside about emergency-room closures has no source and does not fit the evidence well.
  • Phlogiston had real predictive content in its best years. "This belief yielded no advance predictions" is too strong, even though Lavoisier made essentially the essay's complaint about how it was used.

Every item on this list is a citation, a date, a spelling, a number in an illustration, or a flourish of history. None of them is an argument the essays depend on. The book needed a fact-checker. It did not need a different epistemology.

What most of the other notes have in common

The pattern that recurs most often is simple: the objection is answered by a sentence in the essay itself, usually within a paragraph of the passage the note quotes. The following examples are not the only ones:

  • How Much Evidence Does It Take? is charged with never stating the independence assumption. The sentence under the note says "test combinations twice, independently."
  • The Futility of Emergence is charged with ignoring the technical literature. The essay says "There's nothing wrong with saying 'X emerges from Y,' where Y is some specific, detailed model," and quantum Hall quantization is exactly that.
  • Fake Explanations is charged with confusing "false" with "empty." The essay says heat conduction "under normal circumstances" predicts the near side warmer, and it faults the phrase only when it is "used in such fashion," to explain the opposite.
  • Einstein's Arrogance is charged with ignoring Einstein's later mistakes. Its fifth sentence is "If they genuinely deserve criticism, I will not need to wait long for an occasion where they are wrong."
  • Guessing the Teacher's Password is charged with equating understanding with computation. The essay counts a student who "just visualizes something flowing" and holds a match to the plate as having a belief that controls anticipation.
  • Your Strength as a Rationalist is charged with trusting a model that was wrong. The lesson in its title is "Either Your Model Is False Or This Story Is Wrong."
  • Semantic Stopsigns is called unfalsifiable. The essay tests the concept by contrasting how one person treats "liberal democracy" with how he would treat "the Coca-Cola corporation," given the same question.
  • Making History Available is charged with manufacturing availability bias. The essay tells the reader to remember "being a thousand peasants for every ruler."
  • Truly Part of You is charged with resting on self-report. The essay flags that limit itself: "That's harder to boast, without putting it to the test."

A second, smaller pattern is misreading what happens in a parable. In A Fable of Science and Politics, no character shows a backfire effect. All six accept that the sky is blue and then differ in what they do next, which is what the correction literature the note cites actually found. The Bayesian Judo guest moves from "AI is impossible" to "it couldn't be emotional" to "nothing made can be eternal," which is not how someone holding Searle's considered view behaves. The dragon-claimant's excuses always come before the tests, and they are always correct, which is the evidence that he has a model somewhere.

Where Fable lands a real blow

Rhetorical absolutes. "Not one single passage." "Since the days of Socrates." "Always right. Every single time." "A uniquely human flaw." Each absolute gives the skeptic a free counterexample. Most of the underlying claims survive when "always" is reduced to "characteristically," but the essays should have written "characteristically."

Unchecked history, again. The older pass said this, and the finding holds up on a second reading. Book I uses anecdotes the way a speaker uses slides, and at least four of them are wrong. The conclusions rest on the psychology and the mathematics, not on the anecdotes, but a reader should treat any historical illustration in Book I as unverified until checked.

Confident asides from outside the author's field. The emergency-room economics, the Chamberlain letter and the hare's cud are all asserted in passing, with no source, in essays whose subject is not being fooled by confident assertion. None of them affects the main argument, and that is also why they should have been cut.

What stands

The mathematics. Conservation of expected evidence, the conjunction rule, bits as the measure of evidence, and the claim that the evidence needed to locate a hypothesis is its prior improbability. Fable disputes some applications of these results and none of the results themselves.

The psychology the book chose. The biases Book I relies on are conjunction, availability, anchoring, hindsight, the planning fallacy, overconfidence and the curse of knowledge. All of them survived the replication decade, and anchoring and framing replicated across countries in the Many Labs projects. The effects that collapsed in the 2010s, social priming and ego depletion, are not in Book I.

The prescriptions, where they were tested. Each of these has since been tested and holds up:

  • Writing predictions down in advance: after preregistration was required, the share of large heart and lung trials reporting a benefit fell from 57 to 8 percent.
  • Considering the opposite reduces hindsight bias, and that is Hindsight Devalues Science's own exercise.
  • Calibration training measurably improves forecasting, and that is the art Focus Your Uncertainty asks for.
  • Attempting to regenerate knowledge beats rereading it, and that is the regeneration test.
  • Training reduces biases, and the effect persists for months.

The book's claim that a person can be trained to think better now has more evidence behind it than the essays did when they were written.

Verdict

Fable read Book I adversarially and checked its facts, which is valuable, and the corrections listed above should be made. As a critique of the arguments, though, the pass often stopped reading one sentence too early. Of the 161 notes, about a dozen are right, and about a dozen more find a real overstatement. Most of the remainder are answered by a sentence in the essay itself, by the source the note cites, or by a result the note did not mention. The book that emerges is overconfident in its rhetoric and careless with its anecdotes. It is also correct in its mathematics, well chosen in its psychology, and borne out in its practical advice.

The same caveat as the older pass applies. This reply was written as a partisan brief, and a reader who takes Opus, Fable and both Rationalist sets together is better placed than a reader of any single review, this one included.
